Chapter 5 · Trigonometry

Sine, Cosine and Tangent

Every angle comes with three numbers that never change: how far across, how far up, and how steep. They let you find heights you can’t climb and distances you can’t walk.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Use the side ratios of a right triangle: sine = opposite / hypotenuse, cosine = adjacent / hypotenuse, tangent = opposite / adjacent (SOH-CAH-TOA).
  • See why the ratios depend only on the angle (similar triangles), and work backwards from sides to angles.
  • Extend sine and cosine to every angle with the unit circle: cos θ is how far across, sin θ is how far up, with signs that depend on the quadrant.
  • Know the special angles (30°, 45°, 60°) exactly, read tangent as a slope, and use sin²θ + cos²θ = 1.

1Same angle, same shape

Draw a right triangle with a 30° angle. Draw a bigger one with the same 30° angle. The big one is just the small one scaled up: the two are similar triangles, like a photo and its enlargement (1.B.4). So every ratio of sides comes out the same, whatever the size. The angle alone decides the shape.

Name the sides from the angle θ’s point of view: the hypotenuse is the long side across from the right angle, the opposite side is across from θ, and the adjacent side lies next to θ (and isn’t the hypotenuse). The three ratios get names:

SOHsin θ = opposite / hypotenuse
CAHcos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse
TOAtan θ = opposite / adjacent

Working backwards. If you know two sides, the ratio tells you the angle. Calculators have buttons for this: sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹ and tan⁻¹ (say “inverse sine,” and so on) take a ratio and return the angle. A ramp that rises 1 m over 12 m of ground has tan θ = 1/12, so θ = tan⁻¹(1/12) ≈ 4.8°. (Set your calculator to degrees for this page.)

2The unit circle: every angle at once

A right triangle can only hold angles under 90°. To handle any angle, put a point on a circle of radius 1, the unit circle, turned θ counterclockwise from the right-hand side (as in 5.B.1). Drop a line straight down to the horizontal axis and you get a right triangle whose hypotenuse is 1. Its across side is cos θ / 1 = cos θ and its up side is sin θ.

So the point sits at (cos θ, sin θ). That definition keeps working past 90°: on the left half “across” is negative, so cos θ is negative; below the axis “up” is negative, so sin θ is negative.

3Signs in each quadrant

The axes cut the plane into four quadrants, numbered I to IV counterclockwise from the top right. Each one fixes the signs: in I everything is positive; in II only sine (up is still up); in III only tangent (negative ÷ negative); in IV only cosine. The point at 150° is a mirror image of the point at 30°: same height, opposite across. So sin 150° = sin 30° = 1/2 while cos 150° = −cos 30°. The 30° partner of any angle is its reference angle, the angle back to the nearest horizontal axis. Find the values for the reference angle, then fix the signs by quadrant.

4The special angles

Three angles come up so often that their values are worth knowing exactly, and two simple shapes give them all. Cut a square along its diagonal: each half is a 45°-45°-90° triangle. Cut an equilateral triangle (all angles 60°) down the middle: each half is a 30°-60°-90° triangle. Shrink each so the hypotenuse is 1, and Pythagoras (1.B.9) does the rest.

5Tangent is a slope, and a famous identity

Tangent is sin θ / cos θ: up over across, which is the slope of the radius line (rise over run). That’s why the explorer draws it on the line x = 1: follow the radius line until it’s 1 across, and its height there is the slope. At 45° the slope is 1. As θ approaches 90° the line gets steeper without limit, and at exactly 90° it’s vertical: cos θ = 0 and tan θ is undefined.

Every point on the unit circle is distance 1 from the center, so Pythagoras gives the most-used fact in trigonometry, true for every angle:

sin²θ + cos²θ = 1

(sin²θ means (sin θ)².) The readout of the unit-circle explorer checks it for whatever angle you choose.

6Measuring what you can’t reach

Surveyors find the height of a tree without climbing it: walk a measured distance away, measure the angle up to the top, and use tangent. The same right triangle, turned around, gives the length of a shadow from the sun’s height in the sky.

Ladders and ramps use sine and cosine the same way: the ladder is the hypotenuse, the wall height is the opposite side, and the distance out from the wall is the adjacent side.

A Ferris wheel is the unit circle, scaled up and lifted off the ground. A seat at angle θ (counterclockwise from the right-hand spoke) is r cos θ across from the hub and r sin θ above it. As the wheel turns, its height traces the wave of 5.B.4.

7Now, in symbols

\[ \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} \]
SOH-CAH-TOA, for an acute angle θ of a right triangle.
\[ P(\theta) = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta), \qquad \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1 \]
The point at angle θ on the unit circle, and the Pythagorean identity.
\[ \theta = \sin^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}\Big) = \cos^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}\Big) = \tan^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\Big) \]
Working backwards from two sides to the angle.
\[ \sin(90^\circ - \theta) = \cos\theta, \qquad \sin(180^\circ - \theta) = \sin\theta, \qquad \cos(180^\circ - \theta) = -\cos\theta \]
Partners: the complementary angle swaps sine and cosine; the mirror angle in quadrant II keeps the height and flips the across.

In code, the trig functions expect radians, which is the most common slip:

import math                                    # Python's built-in math tools
theta = math.radians(30)                       # convert 30 degrees to radians first (pi/6)
print(math.sin(theta), math.cos(theta))        # 0.5  0.866...  (sin 30° = 1/2, cos 30° = √3/2)
print(math.sin(30))                            # -0.988...: the sine of 30 RADIANS, not 30 degrees
height = 1.6 + 40 * math.tan(math.radians(35)) # eye height + distance × tan(angle up): about 29.6 m
print(round(height, 1))                        # 29.6
print(math.degrees(math.atan(1 / 12)))         # 4.76...: the angle of a ramp rising 1 in 12

9Words to know

Right triangleA triangle with one 90° angle.
HypotenuseThe longest side of a right triangle, across from the right angle.
Opposite and adjacentFor an angle θ: the side across from θ, and the side next to θ that isn’t the hypotenuse.
Sinesin θ = opposite / hypotenuse; on the unit circle, the up coordinate of the point.
Cosinecos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse; on the unit circle, the across coordinate.
Tangenttan θ = opposite / adjacent = sin θ / cos θ: the slope of the radius line. Undefined at 90° and 270°.
Similar trianglesTriangles with the same angles: one is a scaled copy of the other, so their side ratios match.
Inverse trig functionssin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, tan⁻¹: take a ratio and return the angle.
Unit circleThe circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. The point at angle θ is (cos θ, sin θ).
QuadrantOne of the four regions cut out by the axes, numbered I–IV counterclockwise from the top right.
Reference angleThe angle between the radius and the nearest horizontal axis, between 0° and 90°.
Special angles30°, 45° and 60°, whose sine and cosine are 1/2, √2/2 and √3/2 in some order.
Pythagorean identitysin²θ + cos²θ = 1, for every angle.
