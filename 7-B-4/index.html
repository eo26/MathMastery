Chapter 7 · Linear Algebra

A Matrix Is a Move

A 2×2 matrix is just two arrows written side by side: where î lands and where ĵ lands. Those two arrows decide where every other point of the plane goes.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Read a matrix’s columns as the landing spots of î and ĵ.
  • Watch the whole plane move, and see what makes a move linear: the origin stays put, and grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced.
  • Multiply a matrix by a vector by hand, two ways: as x·(column 1) + y·(column 2), and as a stack of dot products.
  • Recognize the basic moves (stretch, shear, rotation, reflection, collapse) and get a first look at how a move changes area.

1Two arrows decide everything

In 7.B.1 you met the two basis vectors: î = [1, 0], one step right, and ĵ = [0, 1], one step up. In 7.B.2 you saw that every point is a mix of them: the point (2, 1) is 2î + 1ĵ, two steps along î and one along ĵ.

Now move the whole plane: stretch it, turn it, tip it over. If the move keeps the origin where it was and keeps grid lines straight, parallel and evenly spaced, then the directions “2 steps along î, 1 step along ĵ” still lead to the same point afterwards, just using the moved arrows. So once you know where î and ĵ land, you know where every point lands.

That’s all a matrix is: the two landing spots written side by side as columns. The matrix 2101 says “î lands at [2, 0] and ĵ lands at [1, 1].” Then (2, 1) = 2î + 1ĵ lands at 2·[2, 0] + 1·[1, 1] = [5, 1].

A move like this is called a linear transformation. Transformation is the mathematician’s word for a move: a rule that takes every point somewhere. Try it below. Drag where î and ĵ land, and watch everything else follow.

2Multiplying a matrix by a vector

Finding where a point lands is called multiplying the matrix by the vector, written Av. There are two ways to do it, and they always agree:

  • The column way (what the picture shows): take x copies of the first column and y copies of the second, and add: x·[a, c] + y·[b, d]. This is a linear combination of the columns, as in 7.B.2.
  • The row way (often fastest by hand): each entry of the answer is a dot product (7.B.3) of one row with the vector. The top entry is a·x + b·y, and the bottom is c·x + d·y.
Example: 1−231 times [2, 1]

3A gallery of moves

Five kinds of moves come up again and again. Each is easy to spot from where î and ĵ land:

  • Stretch (also called scaling): î and ĵ stay on their axes but change length. Equal stretches resize a shape without changing its proportions.
  • Shear: one arrow stays put while the other tips over. Each row slides sideways by an amount proportional to its height, like pushing over a deck of cards.
  • Rotation: both arrows turn by the same angle, keeping length 1 and staying at right angles.
  • Reflection: a mirror flip. The F comes out backwards, and no amount of turning can undo that.
  • Collapse (a projection): î and ĵ land on the same line, so the whole plane is squashed flat.

4What makes a move linear?

Not every move can be written as a matrix. A move is a matrix exactly when it passes three checks:

  • The origin stays put.
  • Straight lines stay straight (including slanted ones, not just the grid lines).
  • Evenly spaced points stay evenly spaced, so the middle of a segment lands in the middle of the moved segment.

Sliding everything over fails the first check. Bending fails the second. An uneven stretch fails the third. None of these can be a 2×2 matrix, because a matrix always sends [0, 0] to [0, 0] and always sends xî + yĵ to x·(where î lands) + y·(where ĵ lands).

Slides are so useful in graphics that programmers fit them into a matrix anyway, with a trick: they add a third coordinate. That trick comes later in the book; a plain 2×2 matrix can never slide.

5How a move changes area (a preview)

Watch the shaded 1-by-1 square in the Matrix Transformer. The move turns it into a parallelogram built on the moved î and ĵ. Every square of the grid becomes an identical copy of that parallelogram, so every area in the plane gets multiplied by the same number: the parallelogram’s area. For the matrix with entries a, b (top row) and c, d (bottom row), it is ad − bc.

A few things to notice in the explorer: a stretch ×2 one way and ×3 the other multiplies area by 6. A shear or a rotation leaves area unchanged (ad − bc = 1). A collapse gives 0, because a flat shape has no area. And when ad − bc is negative, the F comes out mirrored: the move flipped the plane over. This number is the determinant, the star of 7.B.6.

6Now, in symbols

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\,\hat{\imath} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\,\hat{\jmath} = \begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix} \]
The columns are where î and ĵ land.
\[ A\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = x\begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{bmatrix} \]
The column way equals the row way: each entry is a row dotted with the vector.
\[ A(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v}, \qquad A(k\,\mathbf{v}) = k\,A\mathbf{v}, \qquad A\,\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} \]
What “linear” means in symbols: sums and stretches survive the move, and the origin stays put.
\[ \text{area after} = |\,ad - bc\,| \times \text{area before} \]
Preview of the determinant (7.B.6). A negative ad − bc means the plane was flipped.

Graphics software moves a whole picture with one multiplication: put every corner of the picture as a column of one big array, and multiply.

import numpy as np                                    # NumPy: arrays of numbers
t = np.radians(30)                                    # turn by 30 degrees
A = np.array([[np.cos(t), -np.sin(t)],                # top row:    a, b
              [np.sin(t),  np.cos(t)]])               # bottom row: c, d  (columns = where i-hat and j-hat land)
corners = np.array([[0, 2, 2, 0],                     # x of each corner of a 2-by-1 box
                    [0, 0, 1, 1]])                    # y of each corner (one corner per column)
moved = A @ corners                                   # every corner moved by one matrix multiplication
print(moved.round(2))                                 # the turned box, ready to draw

7Where this goes

  • Next (7.B.5): do one move, then another. Two moves in a row are again a single matrix, and the order matters: turning and then stretching is not the same as stretching and then turning.
  • Later in this book: how much a move scales area (the determinant, 7.B.6), how to undo a move (inverse matrices, 7.B.7), arrows that a move only stretches (eigenvectors, Undergraduate tier), and bigger matrices that move 3D space or lists with thousands of entries.
  • In the world: every 3D game and animated film, photo and video editing, robot arms, maps switching between coordinate systems, and the layers of neural networks, which apply matrices to long vectors.

8Words to know

MatrixA rectangular grid of numbers. A 2×2 matrix (2 rows, 2 columns) records a move of the plane.
EntryOne number in a matrix, such as a (top left) or d (bottom right).
Column and rowA column runs top to bottom; a row runs left to right. Column 1 is where î lands, column 2 is where ĵ lands.
Basis vectors î, ĵî = [1, 0] and ĵ = [0, 1]. Every point is a mix xî + yĵ.
TransformationA move or rule that sends every point of the plane to some point.
Linear transformationA move that keeps the origin fixed and keeps lines straight, parallel and evenly spaced. Exactly the moves a matrix can make.
Identity matrixThe matrix with columns [1, 0] and [0, 1]: î and ĵ stay home, so nothing moves.
Matrix–vector productAv: where the point v lands. Equal to x·(column 1) + y·(column 2).
Stretch (scaling)î and ĵ keep their directions but change length.
ShearOne arrow stays put and the other tips over: rows slide sideways in proportion to their height.
RotationA turn around the origin: same lengths, same angles, nothing mirrored.
ReflectionA mirror flip across a line through the origin. Shapes come out backwards.
Collapse (projection)A move that squashes the plane onto a line (or a point). Area becomes 0.
Determinantad − bc: how much the move multiplies areas, negative if it flips. (Explored fully in 7.B.6.)
