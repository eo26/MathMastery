Chapter 9 · Probability

And, Or, Not: Combining Events

“Not”, “or” and “and” build new events out of old ones. See them as cells lighting up on a grid and as overlapping regions, and learn when to subtract, when to add and when to multiply.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Build compound events with and, or and not, and picture them in a Venn diagram and a two-way table.
  • Use the addition rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B), and its shortcut for mutually exclusive events.
  • Use the multiplication rule for independent events, and spot dependent ones where it fails.
  • Use the complement to find the chance of at least one.

1New events from old ones

Roll two dice. “The first die is 6” is an event, call it A. “The second die is 6” is another, B. Joining them with small words makes new events, called compound events:

  • A and B: both happen (double six). The outcomes in both events form their overlap, also called the intersection.
  • A or B: at least one of them happens. In maths “or” is always the inclusive or: A only, B only, or both. All the outcomes together form the union.
  • not A: A doesn’t happen. That’s the complement from 9.B.1. Neither means not A and not B.

When outcomes are equally likely, each compound event is still a count, favourable ÷ total. In the grid below, cells in A only are blue, cells in B only are yellow, and cells in both are green, the mix of the two. Pick what to show, and the cells in that event stay bright.

2“Or”: add, then subtract the overlap

To count “first die is 6 or second die is 6”, you might add: 6 outcomes in A plus 6 in B makes 12. But double six is in both lists, so it was counted twice. Take it away once: 6 + 6 − 1 = 11 outcomes, and P = 11/36. Press Play above to watch this happen. Dividing every count by the total gives the addition rule:

\[ P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B). \]

Sometimes there is no overlap: a card can’t be both red and a club. Events that can’t happen together are mutually exclusive (or disjoint). Their circles in a Venn diagram don’t touch, nothing is counted twice, and the rule shrinks to plain adding: P(red or club) = ½ + ¼ = ¾.

3“And”: multiply, if one doesn’t affect the other

Flip a coin and roll a die. Half the time the coin shows heads, and in ⅙ of those times the die shows 6, so heads-and-six happens in ½ of ⅙ = 1/12 of all tries. That works because the coin can’t influence the die: the events are independent. For independent events there’s a multiplication rule:

\[ \text{independent:}\quad P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B). \]

Many events are dependent: one changes the chance of the other. A traffic jam makes a late bus much more likely, and windy days are more often rainy. Multiplying their probabilities gives the wrong answer. In the explorer below, the area square shows the difference at a glance: the square has area 1, each region’s area is its probability, and the yellow B band runs straight across the square exactly when A and B are independent.

4Two-way tables

Survey results are often given as a two-way table: one event down the side, the other across the top, and totals at the ends. The four inside cells are the same four regions as in the Venn diagram: A and B, A only, B only, neither. Pick “Sport and music” above: 18 of 30 students play a sport, 12 play music, and 7 do both. So 18 + 12 − 7 = 23 do at least one, and 30 − 23 = 7 do neither. The table makes the “subtract the overlap” step hard to forget, because each student sits in exactly one inside cell.

Real survey data is rarely exactly independent. In the survey, P(sport) × P(music) = 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24, while P(both) = 7/30 ≈ 0.233. That’s close, so the two are nearly independent. Whether a small gap like that is real or just chance is a question for statistics (chapter 10).

5“Not” and the “at least one” trick

Roll a die four times. What’s the chance of at least one six? “At least one” covers many cases: one six, two sixes, three, four. Its complement is just one case: no sixes. Each roll misses 6 with probability ⅚, and the rolls are independent, so

\[ P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - P(\text{no sixes}) = 1 - \left(\tfrac56\right)^4 \approx 0.518. \]

Slightly better than even. Adding ⅙ four times gives ⅔, which is far too big, and with six rolls it would claim a six is certain. A quick simulation settles the argument:

import random                                          # Python's random-number tools
trials = 100_000                                       # how many times to repeat the experiment
hits = 0                                               # experiments with at least one six
for _ in range(trials):                                # repeat the experiment many times
    rolls = [random.randint(1, 6) for _ in range(4)]   # roll a fair die four times
    if 6 in rolls:                                     # did any of the four rolls show a 6?
        hits += 1                                      # yes: count this experiment
print(hits / trials)                                   # simulated chance: about 0.518
print(1 - (5/6)**4)                                    # the complement rule: 0.5177...
print(4 * (1/6))                                       # the adding mistake: 0.667, far too big

The same trick explains why backups work. If each of three independent alarm clocks fails 1 time in 10, the chance all three fail is 0.1³ = 0.001, so at least one rings with probability 0.999.

6Traps

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ Counting the overlap twice

P(heart or face card) is not 13/52 + 12/52. The jack, queen and king of hearts are in both groups. Subtract them once: 13 + 12 − 3 = 22, so P = 22/52.

✕ Adding for “at least one”

Adding ⅙ per roll says 6 rolls make a six certain, and 7 rolls make it more than certain. The true chance, 1 − (⅚)ⁿ, creeps toward 1 but never reaches it.

✕ Multiplying dependent events

P(traffic jam) = 0.25 and P(bus late) = 0.2, but 0.25 × 0.2 = 0.05 is wrong: jams cause late buses, so both happen 15% of the time. Multiply only when the events are independent.

7Now, in symbols

Set notation from chapter 2 gives short names: A ∩ B (“A intersect B”) for A and B, A ∪ B (“A union B”) for A or B, and Aᶜ or A′ for not A. Counts use |A|, as in 9.B.1.

\[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|, \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]
\[ \text{mutually exclusive: } P(A \cap B) = 0, \quad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \]
\[ \text{independent: } P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) \]
\[ P(A^{c}) = 1 - P(A), \qquad P(\text{at least one success in } n \text{ independent tries}) = 1 - (1-p)^{n} \]

Careful with the last pair of words: mutually exclusive events (with nonzero chances) are never independent. If A happens, B can’t, so knowing about A changes everything about B.

9Words to know

Compound eventAn event built from others with and, or, not.
A and BBoth happen. Also called the intersection, A ∩ B.
Overlap / intersectionThe outcomes in both events.
A or BAt least one happens: A only, B only, or both. Also called the union, A ∪ B.
Inclusive or“Or” that includes both. In maths, “or” always means this.
UnionAll outcomes in A, in B, or in both.
Complement (not A)Every outcome not in A. P(not A) = 1 − P(A).
NeitherNot A and not B: outside both circles.
Venn diagramOverlapping circles inside a box (the sample space) showing how events overlap.
Two-way tableA table with one event down the side and one across the top, with totals.
Addition ruleP(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B).
Mutually exclusive (disjoint)Events that can’t happen together: P(A and B) = 0.
Independent eventsOne doesn’t change the chance of the other.
Dependent eventsOne changes the chance of the other.
Multiplication ruleFor independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
At least oneOne or more. Easiest found as 1 − P(none).
Area squareA square of area 1 split into regions whose areas are the probabilities.
