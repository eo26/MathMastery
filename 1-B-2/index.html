Multiplication as Stretching

Adding slides the whole number line. Multiplying stretches it, like a rubber band pinned at 0. That one picture explains why ×½ shrinks, why ×0 collapses everything, and why a negative times a negative is positive.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • See multiplying by m as stretching the whole line around 0, which never moves.
  • See ×3 as three copies laid end to end: repeated adding and stretching are the same thing.
  • Understand shrinking (×½), doing nothing (×1), collapsing (×0) and flipping (×−1).
  • Tell a slide from a stretch, and explain why (−) × (−) = +.

1Three copies of 2

You probably first met 3 × 2 as “three groups of 2”: 2 + 2 + 2 = 6. On the number line from 1.B.1, that’s three hops of 2, laid end to end, starting at 0. The result lands at 6.

Now picture the whole line made of rubber, pinned down at 0. Grab it and stretch it so that every distance from 0 becomes three times as long. The point that was at 2 ends up at 6, because 6 is three times as far from 0. Stretching by 3 and laying down three copies give exactly the same answer. The stretch picture has one big advantage: it keeps working for numbers like ½ and −1, where “three groups of” stops making sense.

The number you stretch by is called the stretch factor. We’ll call it m (for multiplier), the starting point a, and where it lands b.

Try every preset, then look for these things:

  1. 0 never moves. It’s the pin. Every other point moves away from 0 (or toward it), but 0 stays exactly where it was. This is the big difference from adding.
  2. Spacing changes, evenly. After ×2, every gap between ticks is twice as wide, everywhere. The line stretches the same amount all along its length.
  3. Multiplying can make things smaller. ×½ squeezes every point halfway toward 0. Any stretch factor between 0 and 1 shrinks.
  4. ×1 does nothing, ×0 collapses everything. ×1 is the do-nothing stretch. ×0 squashes the whole line onto the single point 0: every number times 0 is 0.
  5. A negative stretch flips. ×−1 sends every point to its mirror image on the other side of 0. ×−2 flips and doubles.

2Slide or stretch?

In 1.B.1, adding slid the whole line: every point moved the same distance, and 0 moved too. Multiplying stretches the line: 0 stays pinned, and the farther a point is from 0, the farther it moves. Run both on the same starting point and compare:

    3Why a negative times a negative is positive

    Here’s the rule people memorize without understanding: (−) × (−) = +. The stretch picture makes it obvious. A negative stretch factor flips the line around 0. So:

    • Positive × positive: no flips. A point on the right stays on the right. 3 × 2 = 6.
    • Negative × positive (or positive × negative): one flip. The point crosses to the other side. 3 × (−2) = −6 and (−3) × 2 = −6.
    • Negative × negative: the point starts on the left, and the flip sends it to the right. (−3) × (−2) = 6. Two flips put you back facing the way you started.
    ×by a positiveby a negative
    a positive+ (no flip)− (one flip)
    a negative− (already flipped)+ (flipped back)

    Try the “(−) × (−)” preset in the explorer: the point starts at −3, the line flips, and it lands at +6.

    4Now, in symbols

    In the explorer the start was labeled a, the stretch factor m, and the landing spot on the ruler b:

    \[ a \times m = b \]
    Start at \(a\), stretch by \(m\), land on \(b\). With the first preset: \(3 \times 2 = 6\).

    The whole line moves, so we can write the stretch as a rule for every point \(x\), just like the slide \(x \mapsto x + s\) from 1.B.1:

    \[ x \;\longmapsto\; m \cdot x \qquad\text{and}\qquad 0 \;\longmapsto\; m \cdot 0 = 0 \]
    A stretch always leaves 0 where it is. A slide never does (unless it’s a slide of 0).

    And the flips, written as rules:

    \[ (-1) \cdot x = -x, \qquad (-1)\cdot(-1) = 1 \]
    Flipping twice is the same as not flipping at all.

    5Where this goes

    • Next (1.B.3): stretching by ½ squeezed every point halfway to 0. That’s where fractions come from, and what lives in the gaps between whole numbers.
    • Later in this book: the constant of proportionality k in 1.B.5 is a stretch factor: y = k · x stretches the x line into the y line. In Linear Algebra, a matrix is a stretch (and turn, and flip) of the whole plane instead of a line (7.B.4), and the “0 never moves” rule is what makes those moves linear.
    • In the world: zooming a photo or a map in and out, making a scale drawing, doubling a recipe, or turning a thermostat that runs “2 times as fast.” Every zoom is a stretch around a fixed center.

    6Words to know

    MultiplyStretch the whole number line around 0 by a stretch factor. Also: add copies, when the factor is a whole number.
    FactorEach number being multiplied. In 3 × 2, both 3 and 2 are factors.
    ProductThe result of multiplying. The product of 3 and 2 is 6.
    Stretch factorThe number m that every distance from 0 gets multiplied by. Bigger than 1 stretches; between 0 and 1 shrinks.
    Repeated addition3 × 2 = 2 + 2 + 2: three copies laid end to end.
    Identity (×1)Multiplying by 1 leaves every number unchanged: the do-nothing stretch.
    Zero productAny number times 0 is 0: the whole line collapses onto 0.
    Fixed pointA point a move leaves where it is. For every stretch, 0 is a fixed point.
