Chapter 7 · Linear Algebra

Inverse Matrices

Every move that doesn’t squash the plane has an undo move, and finding it lets you run any matrix backwards.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • See the inverse A−1 as the move that brings every point back where it started, so A−1A = I.
  • Find a 2×2 inverse in three steps: swap a and d, change the signs of b and c, divide by the determinant.
  • Know when there is no inverse: when det = 0, the move is singular and can’t be undone.
  • Undo two moves in reverse order, (BA)−1 = A−1B−1, and run a matrix backwards to solve Ax = b.

1Every good move has an undo

With numbers, ×3 is undone by ×⅓: triple something, then take a third, and you’re back where you started. The number ⅓ is the inverse of 3, because ⅓ × 3 = 1, and multiplying by 1 changes nothing.

Matrices work the same way. A matrix A moves every point of the plane (7.B.4). Its inverse matrix, written A−1 and read “A inverse,” is the move that puts every point back. Doing A and then A−1 is the same as not moving at all, which is the identity matrix I, the matrix version of ×1:

A−1A = I  and  AA−1 = I

A few things to notice as you click through the presets:

  • The undo of a stretch is a shrink, the undo of a turn is the opposite turn, and the undo of a shear leans the other way. A mirror is its own undo: mirror twice and you’re home.
  • If A multiplies every area by det A (7.B.6), the undo must divide every area by the same amount, so det(A−1) = 1 / det A.
  • “Almost flat” squeezes the plane into a thin sliver, so its undo has to pull hard, and its entries are big. “Collapse” squeezes it all the way flat, and then nothing can pull it back: three different start points (1, 2, 3) land on the same spot, and the undo would have to send one spot to three places at once.

A matrix that has an inverse is invertible. One that doesn’t (det = 0) is singular.

3The 2×2 formula: swap, change signs, divide

For a 2×2 matrix the undo can always be written down in three steps. Start with a, b on top and c, d underneath:

  1. Swap a and d (the main diagonal trades places).
  2. Change the signs of b and c (they stay where they are).
  3. Divide every entry by the determinant, det = ad − bc.

Why does that work? Multiply A by the matrix from steps 1 and 2 and something tidy falls out: ad − bc on the diagonal and 0 everywhere else. That’s det × I, a plain zoom by the determinant. Step 3 divides the zoom away, leaving I. Step through it with your own numbers:

Step 3 is where a singular matrix gets stuck: when det = 0 there’s nothing to divide by. That matches the picture. A det of 0 means the plane was squashed flat, and a squash can’t be undone. So a 2×2 matrix is invertible exactly when det ≠ 0. Since the undo divides areas by det A, det(A−1) = 1/det A.

One warning: the inverse is not “1 over each entry.” The inverse of the matrix with rows 2, 1 and 1, 1 is the matrix with rows 1, −1 and −1, 2, not ½, 1, 1, 1. Undoing a move means undoing the whole move at once, and the entries are tangled together.

4Socks and shoes: undo in reverse order

In the morning you put on your socks, then your shoes. At night you undo that, but not in the same order: shoes come off first, then socks. The last thing you did is the first thing you undo.

Moves are the same. If you do A first and then B, the combined move is BA (the right-hand matrix acts first, 7.B.5). To undo it, take off B first, then A: do B−1 first and then A−1. Written as one matrix that’s A−1B−1, so

(BA)−1 = A−1B−1.

This is the reverse-order rule. Below, the third panel undoes in reverse order and the F lands home. The fourth tries the same order as before, and usually misses.

5Secret codes

Here’s a classic use of a matrix and its inverse. Turn letters into numbers (A = 1, B = 2, …, Z = 26, a space = 0), group them in pairs, and multiply each pair by a key matrix K. Out come numbers that look like nonsense. Your friend, who knows the key, multiplies by K−1 and gets the message back. Encoding is K, decoding is its undo.

Choosing a key takes care. A key with det = 0 sends different pairs to the same code numbers, so even someone who knows the key can’t tell which pair was meant. Try the bad key.

6Running a matrix backwards: solving Ax = b

Most of the time a matrix tells you where things go: start at x, and A sends you to b = Ax. Often the real question is backwards: you know where you ended up, b, and want to know where you started. That’s solving the equation Ax = b for the unknown vector x, and the inverse answers it in one step:

x = A−1b.

It’s the same move as solving 3x = 12 by multiplying both sides by ⅓. You already did it in “Do, then undo”: dragging b in the middle panel moved its start x = A−1b. Here’s a kitchen version.

A smoothie bar makes two drinks. A Berry Blast takes 2 cups of strawberries and 1 banana. A Tropical takes 1 cup of strawberries and 3 bananas. Put each recipe in a column and you have a matrix R. If the bar makes n1 Berry Blasts and n2 Tropicals, the fruit used is Rn (7.B.4). At closing time the owner only knows how much fruit is gone. How many of each drink were sold? Run R backwards.

Every system of two linear equations in two unknowns can be written as one matrix equation Ax = b. That’s the subject of the next section (7.B.8), and it’s why “does A have an inverse?” and “does the system have exactly one solution?” are the same question.

7Now, in symbols

\[ A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]
The inverse undoes the move from either side.
\[ \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix} \qquad (ad - bc \neq 0) \]
Swap a and d, change the signs of b and c, divide by the determinant.
\[ \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det A}, \qquad (A^{-1})^{-1} = A, \qquad (BA)^{-1} = A^{-1}B^{-1} \]
The undo divides areas; undoing the undo is the move itself; chained moves are undone in reverse order.
\[ A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} \]
When A is invertible, the system has exactly one solution.

For bigger matrices there’s no short formula. A computer uses row reduction (7.B.9). In practice it rarely builds A−1 at all: to solve Ax = b it goes straight for x, which is faster and more accurate:

import numpy as np                              # NumPy: arrays of numbers
R = np.array([[2, 1], [1, 3]])                  # smoothie recipes as columns
used = np.array([11, 13])                       # cups of strawberries, bananas used today
print(np.linalg.inv(R))                         # [[ 0.6 -0.2] [-0.2  0.4]] = (1/5)[[3, -1], [-1, 2]]
print(np.linalg.inv(R) @ used)                  # [4. 3.]: 4 Berry Blasts, 3 Tropicals
print(np.linalg.solve(R, used))                 # [4. 3.] again, without forming the inverse (preferred)
bad = np.array([[2, 4], [1, 2]])                # det = 2*2 - 4*1 = 0
try:
    np.linalg.inv(bad)                          # a singular matrix has no inverse...
except np.linalg.LinAlgError as e:
    print(e)                                    # ...so NumPy says "Singular matrix"

8Where this goes

  • Next (7.B.8): writing a whole system of equations as one matrix equation Ax = b, and reading from det A whether it has one solution, none, or infinitely many.
  • Later in this book: row reduction finds inverses of any size (7.B.9), 3×3 inverses undo moves of space (7.B.10), and the Undergraduate tier uses inverses to change coordinates (change of basis) and to undo transformations in calculus.
  • In the world: video games and phone cameras undo moves to work out where a pixel came from, GPS solves equations like Ax = b to pin down your position, and engineers solve huge systems of this kind to design bridges and circuits.

9Words to know

Inverse matrixA−1: the move that undoes A, so A−1A = AA−1 = I.
Identity matrixI: the “do nothing” move, with 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere. The matrix version of ×1.
InvertibleHas an inverse. A 2×2 matrix is invertible exactly when det ≠ 0.
SingularHas no inverse: det = 0, so the move squashes the plane flat and different points land on the same spot.
Swap, change signs, divideThe 2×2 inverse: swap a and d, negate b and c, divide by ad − bc.
Reverse-order rule(BA)−1 = A−1B−1: undo the last move first, like shoes before socks.
Key (in a code)The matrix K that encodes a message. Decoding uses K−1, so a key must be invertible.
Unknown vectorThe x in Ax = b: the start point you’re solving for. If A is invertible, x = A−1b.
