Complex Numbers and Polynomial Roots

No real number squares to −1, so we invent one, i, and the number line opens up into a plane. In that plane multiplying means turning, and every polynomial has exactly as many roots as its degree.

1A number whose square is −1

The equation x² = −1 has no real solution: every real number squared is 0 or positive (3.B.10). Mathematicians did what they did when they invented negative numbers and fractions: they made up a new number with the property they wanted. Call it i, the imaginary unit, and declare

\[ i^2 = -1. \]

Then numbers like 3 + 2i make sense: a complex number a + bi has a real part a and an imaginary part b. Ordinary real numbers are the ones with b = 0, and numbers like 2i, with a = 0, are called imaginary (an unlucky name: they are no less real than −5).

Where do they live? The real numbers already fill a line, so i goes off the line: one unit straight up. Then a + bi is the point (a, b) of the complex plane: the real axis runs across, the imaginary axis runs up. Here’s why “up” is right. Multiplying by −1 turns a number half a turn (180°) around 0: 3 goes to −3. Multiplying by i twice must do the same, since i · i = −1, so multiplying by i once is a quarter turn, 90°. And four quarter turns get you back where you started: i, i² = −1, i³ = −i, i⁴ = 1.

2Arithmetic as moves in the plane

Adding works part by part: (3 + 2i) + (1 − 4i) = 4 − 2i. In the plane it’s sliding one arrow onto the tip of the other, exactly like adding vectors.

Multiplying uses the distributive property and i² = −1: (2 + i)(1 + 2i) = 2 + 4i + i + 2i² = 2 + 5i − 2 = 5i. In the plane something remarkable happens: the angles add and the lengths multiply. The length of a + bi is its modulus |a + bi| = √(a² + b²) (Pythagoras), and its angle up from the positive real axis is its argument. So multiplying by a number of length 1 is a pure turn, and that’s how computers rotate things.

Flipping the sign of the imaginary part gives the conjugate: the conjugate of 3 + 4i is 3 − 4i, its mirror image in the real axis. A number times its conjugate is always a positive real number: (3 + 4i)(3 − 4i) = 9 + 16 = 25 = |3 + 4i|². That’s the trick for dividing: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom, just like rationalizing a denominator in 3.B.10.

3Quadratics with no real roots

In 3.B.9 the quadratic formula stalled when the discriminant D = b² − 4ac was negative: √D wasn’t a real number. Now it is. √(−16) = √16 · √(−1) = 4i. So x² − 4x + 13 = 0 has

\[ x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2} = 2 \pm 3i. \]

Check one: (2 + 3i)² − 4(2 + 3i) + 13 = (4 + 12i − 9) − 8 − 12i + 13 = 0 ✓. The two roots are conjugates, h ± ki, and their real part h = 2 is the parabola’s axis of symmetry. Picture it: as a parabola is lifted off the x-axis, its two real roots slide together, meet at the vertex (D = 0), and then split sideways out of the real line, one straight up and one straight down in the complex plane.

4Every polynomial has all its roots

With complex numbers allowed, factoring never gets stuck. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra says that every polynomial of degree n ≥ 1 has exactly n complex roots, counting multiplicity (3.B.8), so it factors completely into n linear factors a(x − r₁)(x − r₂)⋯(x − rn). Gauss gave the first widely accepted proof in 1799.

If the coefficients are real, the non-real roots come in conjugate pairs a ± bi: the picture of the roots is symmetric about the real axis. Each pair multiplies to a real quadratic, (x − (a + bi))(x − (a − bi)) = x² − 2ax + (a² + b²). Two consequences: the graph only shows the real roots as x-intercepts, the rest are “hidden” off the line; and a real polynomial of odd degree always has at least one real root, since the pairs can’t use up an odd number.

5Complex numbers at work

Complex numbers are a tool for anything that turns or oscillates. Game and graphics engines rotate shapes by multiplying by a complex number of length 1. Electrical engineers write a circuit’s resistance to alternating current as a complex number, so that the delay between voltage and current is just an angle. And whether a spring bounces or slowly settles depends on whether the roots of a quadratic are complex.

6Traps and non-examples

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ √(−4) · √(−9) = √36 = 6

Write each with i first: √(−4) = 2i and √(−9) = 3i, so the product is 6i² = −6. The rule √a·√b = √(ab) only holds when a, b ≥ 0. In the plane: two quarter turns make a half turn.

✕ (2 + 3i)² = 4 + 9 = 13

Two mistakes in one: the middle term 2·2·3i = 12i is missing (3.B.8), and (3i)² = 9i² = −9, not +9. Correct: 4 + 12i − 9 = −5 + 12i.

✕ x² + 4 = 0 has no roots

It has no real roots: the parabola misses the x-axis. It has two complex roots, ±2i, straight up and down from the axis of symmetry. Degree 2, two roots.
