Quadratics and Parabolas

Every quadratic graph is the same U-shape, stretched, flipped and slid around. Three ways of writing it each show off a different feature, and one formula finds where it crosses zero, every time.

1One shape, moved around

A quadratic function is a polynomial of degree 2, like y = 2x² − 12x + 19. Its graph is a parabola: the U-shape of y = x², the path of a thrown ball, the curve of a fountain’s jet. Every parabola is y = x² changed in only three ways.

  • Stretch or flip with a. y = 2x² doubles every height, so it looks narrower; y = ½x² halves them and looks wider. A negative a flips the U upside down: it opens downward.
  • Slide sideways with h. y = (x − 3)² is y = x² moved 3 to the right: it takes the value 0 at x = 3 instead of 0.
  • Slide up or down with k. Adding k lifts every point by k.

The turning point is the vertex (h, k). If the parabola opens up, the vertex is its lowest point, a minimum; if it opens down, it’s the highest point, a maximum. The vertical line x = h through the vertex is the axis of symmetry: the two halves are mirror images, so the two roots (if there are any) sit at equal distances on either side of it.

2Three forms, three features

The same parabola can be written three ways, and each one makes a different feature easy to read.

  • Standard form y = ax² + bx + c. Put x = 0 and you get y = c: the y-intercept. The vertex is hidden, but it’s at x = −b/(2a).
  • Vertex form y = a(x − h)² + k. The vertex (h, k) is right there. Mind the sign: (x − 3)² has h = 3 and (x + 3)² has h = −3.
  • Factored form y = a(x − r₁)(x − r₂). The roots r₁ and r₂ are right there (3.B.8), and the vertex is halfway between them. A parabola that never touches the x-axis has no factored form with real numbers.

Going from vertex or factored form to standard form is just multiplying out. Going the other way takes some work: factoring (3.B.8) gives the factored form, and completing the square gives the vertex form.

3Completing the square

Take x² + 6x + 5 as tiles. Split the six x tiles in half: three down the right side of the x² square, three along the bottom. That’s almost a bigger square, (x + 3)² — only the 3 × 3 corner is missing. So add the 9 corner tiles, and take 9 away again so nothing has changed:

\[ x^2 + 6x + 5 = (x^2 + 6x + 9) - 9 + 5 = (x+3)^2 - 4. \]

Now it’s in vertex form: the vertex is (−3, −4). In general you add (b/2)², half the x coefficient, squared. If a isn’t 1, take it out of the first two terms first: 2x² − 12x + 19 = 2(x² − 6x) + 19 = 2[(x − 3)² − 9] + 19 = 2(x − 3)² + 1.

    4The quadratic formula

    Factoring only works when the roots are nice. Completing the square works every time, and doing it once with letters instead of numbers gives a formula for the roots of any ax² + bx + c = 0:

    \[ ax^2+bx+c=0 \;\Rightarrow\; x^2+\tfrac{b}{a}x = -\tfrac{c}{a} \;\Rightarrow\; \left(x+\tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2} \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}. \]

    Read it as a picture: −b/(2a) is the axis of symmetry, and the roots sit √(b² − 4ac)/(2|a|) to either side of it. The ± is there because a square has two square roots: x² = 9 means x = 3 or x = −3.

    The number under the root, D = b² − 4ac, is the discriminant. If D > 0 there are two real roots; if D = 0, the two roots meet at the vertex (a double root), so the parabola just touches the axis; if D < 0, there’s no real square root and no real roots: the parabola misses the axis entirely. (3.B.15 shows the roots are still there, as complex numbers.)

    6Traps and non-examples

    Compare

    Easy to get wrong

    ✕ (x − 3)² moves left

    (x − 3)² is zero when x = 3, so the vertex moves to x = +3: right. Inside the bracket, the shift looks backwards; outside (+k), it doesn’t.

    ✕ x² = 9, so x = 3

    The line y = 9 meets the parabola twice: (−3)² = 9 too. Taking a square root gives ±, which is where the ± in the formula comes from.

    ✕ x² = 5x, divide by x: x = 5

    Dividing by x quietly assumes x ≠ 0 and loses a root. Move everything over and factor: x(x − 5) = 0, so x = 0 or 5.

    7Now, in symbols

    \[ y = ax^2+bx+c \;=\; a(x-h)^2+k \;=\; a(x-r_1)(x-r_2), \qquad a \ne 0 \]
    \[ h = -\frac{b}{2a}, \qquad k = c - \frac{b^2}{4a}, \qquad h = \frac{r_1+r_2}{2} \]
    \[ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}, \qquad D = b^2 - 4ac:\quad D>0 \text{ two roots},\ D=0 \text{ one (double) root},\ D<0 \text{ no real roots} \]
    \[ r_1 + r_2 = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad r_1 r_2 = \frac{c}{a}, \qquad y = \frac{x^2}{4f} \text{ has focus } (0, f) \text{ and directrix } y = -f \]

    9Words to know

    Quadratic functionA function y = ax² + bx + c with a ≠ 0.
    ParabolaThe U-shaped graph of a quadratic.
    VertexThe turning point (h, k) of a parabola.
    Axis of symmetryThe vertical line x = h through the vertex; the two halves mirror each other.
    Maximum / minimumThe highest / lowest value; for a parabola, the y of the vertex.
    Opens up / downa > 0 gives a U (minimum); a < 0 gives an upside-down U (maximum).
    Standard formy = ax² + bx + c; shows the y-intercept c.
    Vertex formy = a(x − h)² + k; shows the vertex.
    Factored formy = a(x − r₁)(x − r₂); shows the roots.
    y-interceptWhere the graph crosses the y-axis: (0, c).
    RootsThe x-values where y = 0; the x-intercepts.
    Completing the squareAdding and subtracting (b/2)² to turn x² + bx into a perfect square.
    Quadratic formulax = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a).
    DiscriminantD = b² − 4ac; its sign says how many real roots there are.
    Double rootA root that appears twice; the parabola touches the axis at its vertex.
    FocusThe point (0, f) inside y = x²/(4f) where reflected parallel rays meet.
    DirectrixThe line y = −f; every point of the parabola is as far from it as from the focus.
