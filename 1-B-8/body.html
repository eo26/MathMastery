Proportional Reasoning: Solving Problems

Recipes, maps, prices and speeds are all the same problem in different clothes. Check that the situation really is proportional, then pick any of three tools. They always agree.

1The toolkit

Every page in this group gave you a tool. Here they are side by side:

  1. Find the one (unit rate). Divide to get the amount for exactly 1, the constant of proportionality k, then multiply up to what you need (1.B.5).
  2. Ratio table or double number line. Scale both columns by the same number, in friendly steps, until you reach the row you want (1.B.4).
  3. Proportion. Write two equal ratios with a ? for the unknown, then solve. Cross products make it quick: if ab = cd, then a × d = b × c (1.B.4).

But before any of them, ask the question that keeps you out of trouble: is this really a proportional relationship? If doubling one amount doesn’t double the other, none of these tools apply. Section 4 is full of situations like that.

2The better buy

Stores sell the same thing in different sizes, and the bigger box isn’t always the better deal. To compare fairly, find the price of one unit of each: the unit price. It’s the constant of proportionality between amount and cost, in dollars per ounce (or per egg, or per pound). The lower unit price is the better buy.

3Two steps in a row

Some problems need two proportional steps, one after the other. A trail map says 1 inch = 2 miles. The trail is 3 inches long on the map, and you hike at 2.5 miles per hour. How long will the hike take?

3 in→× 2 mi per in→6 mi→÷ 2.5 mi per h→2.4 h

Each arrow is one of the machines from 1.B.5. The first converts map inches to real miles (multiply by the scale). The second turns miles into hours (divide by the speed, since you know miles and want hours). Then 2.4 hours is 2 hours and 0.4 × 60 = 24 minutes. Keep track of the units after every step, and you’ll always know whether to multiply or divide next.

4Can you scale it?

Proportional reasoning is powerful, and that’s what makes it dangerous: it will happily give you a confident wrong answer if the situation isn’t proportional. Before scaling, check: does zero go with zero? And does doubling one amount double the other? Decide for each situation below.

5Now, in symbols

In the explorer, the known pair was (\(x_0\), \(y_0\)) and the unknown was the ?. Method A finds \(k\) and uses the equation for a proportional relationship:

\[ k = \frac{y_0}{x_0}, \qquad y = k \cdot x, \qquad x = \frac{y}{k} \]
The constant of proportionality \(k\) is the unit rate: how much \(y\) for one \(x\).

Method C writes a proportion and solves it with cross products:

\[ \frac{y_0}{x_0} = \frac{y}{x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_0 \cdot x = x_0 \cdot y \]
Recipe example: \(\tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{?}{10}\), so \(4 \cdot ? = 3 \cdot 10 = 30\), and \(? = 7.5\) cups.

Method B is the same idea done in steps: every row of a ratio table is \((n\,x_0,\; n\,y_0)\) for some multiplier \(n\).

7Words to know

Proportional relationshipTwo amounts linked by y = k · x: zero goes with zero and doubling one doubles the other. Check this before scaling anything.
Constant of proportionalityThe unit rate k = y ÷ x: cups per person, miles per inch, dollars per ounce.
ProportionAn equation saying two ratios are equal, like 3/4 = 7.5/10. Solve one with cross products.
Unit rateThe amount for exactly one: 60 miles per 1 hour.
Unit priceThe cost of one unit: dollars per ounce. The lower unit price is the better buy.
ScaleThe ratio between a map or model and the real thing, like 1 inch = 5 miles or 1 : 24.
Cross productsIn a/b = c/d, the products a × d and b × c. They’re equal exactly when the ratios are equal.
Inverse proportionWhen one amount goes up as the other goes down in a matched way (more painters, less time). Not proportional, so don’t scale it directly.
