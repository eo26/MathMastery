Polynomials and Factoring

Multiplying polynomials is finding the area of a rectangle. Factoring runs it backwards and finds the rectangle’s sides, and those sides tell you exactly where the graph crosses zero.

1What a polynomial is

A polynomial is what you can build from a variable x and numbers using only adding, subtracting and multiplying: things like 3x² + 2x − 5. Each piece joined by + or − is a term. The number in front of a term is its coefficient (3 and 2 here), and the term with no x, −5, is the constant term. No dividing by x, no square roots of x, and every exponent is a whole number 0, 1, 2, …

The highest power of x is the degree: 3x² + 2x − 5 has degree 2, and its leading coefficient is 3, the coefficient of that highest power. Writing the terms from the highest power down is standard form. Short polynomials have names by how many terms they have, a monomial (one term, 7x³), a binomial (two, x + 4) and a trinomial (three), and by degree: degree 1 is linear, degree 2 is a quadratic, degree 3 is a cubic.

To add or subtract polynomials, combine like terms: terms with the same power of x. Think of tiles. A blue square is x², a green bar is x, a small orange square is 1, and a red tile of the same shape is the negative. A tile and its negative make a zero pair and cancel. You can add 2 blue squares to 1 blue square, but never a blue square to a green bar: x² and x are different shapes.

2Multiplying is area

A rectangle that is x + 3 wide and x + 2 tall has area (x + 3)(x + 2). Cut it along the joins and you get four pieces: an x by x square, two strips of x tiles, and a corner of unit tiles. Add the pieces: x² + 2x + 3x + 6 = x² + 5x + 6. That is the distributive property: every piece of one side multiplies every piece of the other. Writing it out to standard form is called expanding.

For two binomials, the four pieces are often remembered as FOIL: First, Outer, Inner, Last. The rectangle is better than the word, because it also works for a trinomial times a binomial (six pieces) or anything bigger. A negative side just makes negative tiles, and negative × negative = positive in the corner.

3Factoring is multiplying backwards

To factor a polynomial is to write it as a product, like finding the sides of a rectangle when you only know its tiles. Always start by taking out the greatest common factor (GCF): 3x² − 12 = 3(x² − 4), and 2x² + 6x = 2x(x + 3).

For x² + bx + c, look back at the rectangle (x + m)(x + n): the corner holds m·n unit tiles and the two strips hold m + n x tiles. So you need two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. For x² + 5x + 6: 2 · 3 = 6 and 2 + 3 = 5, so (x + 2)(x + 3).

When the leading coefficient a isn’t 1, look for two numbers that multiply to a·c and add to b, split the middle term with them, and use factoring by grouping: take a common factor out of each pair of terms, and a shared bracket appears. Some polynomials have no such pair, like x² + 2x + 5. They are called prime (or irreducible) over the whole numbers: their tiles can’t be arranged into a rectangle.

4Patterns worth knowing by sight

Three shapes come up so often that it pays to recognise them at a glance.

  • Difference of squares. a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b). Cut a small square out of a big one, slide one piece round, and the L-shape becomes a rectangle. The two middle strips cancel (+ab and −ab), which is why there’s no middle term.
  • Perfect square trinomial. (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b², and (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b². The square has two ab strips as well as the two squares.
  • Sum and difference of cubes. a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²) and a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²). Take a small cube out of the corner of a big one, and what’s left splits into three slabs, each a − b thick.

The same patterns make mental arithmetic easy: 43 × 37 = (40 + 3)(40 − 3) = 1600 − 9 = 1591, and 51² = 2500 + 100 + 1 = 2601.

5Factors tell you the roots

If two numbers multiply to zero, at least one of them is zero. That is the zero-product property, and it’s why factoring matters. (x − 2)(x + 3) = 0 only when x − 2 = 0 or x + 3 = 0, so x = 2 or x = −3. These values are the roots (or zeros) of the polynomial, and they are the x-intercepts of its graph. Watch the sign: the factor x + 3 gives the root −3.

A factor can appear more than once. In (x − 1)²(x + 2), the root 1 has multiplicity 2. At a root of odd multiplicity the graph crosses the axis; at even multiplicity it touches and turns back; multiplicity 3 crosses while flattening out. Far from the roots only the leading term matters, axn, and it fixes the end behavior: an even degree sends both ends the same way, an odd degree sends them opposite ways, and a negative leading coefficient flips the picture.

7Traps and non-examples

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ (a + b)² = a² + b²

The square of side a + b has four pieces. Dropping the two ab strips (red) loses area: (3 + 2)² = 25, not 9 + 4 = 13.

✕ x² + 9 = (x + 3)(x − 3)

(x + 3)(x − 3) = x² − 9. A sum of squares x² + 9 is never zero (its graph never meets the axis), so it has no real factors at all.

✕ (x − 1)(x − 4) = 10 ⇒ x − 1 = 10

Only zero forces a factor to be zero; 10 = 2 × 5 = 1 × 10 = … Move the 10 over first: x² − 5x − 6 = (x − 6)(x + 1) = 0, so x = 6 or −1 (gold), not 11 or 14.

Past degree 2 or 3, factoring by hand gets hard, and many polynomials have no whole-number roots at all. A computer can find roots numerically, or factor exactly:

import numpy as np                          # numerical tools
print(np.roots([4, -100, 600, 0]))          # roots of the box volume 4x³ − 100x² + 600x: [15. 10. 0.]

from sympy import symbols, factor, expand   # exact algebra on symbols
x = symbols('x')                            # a symbol to build expressions with
print(factor(6*x**2 + x - 2))               # (2*x - 1)*(3*x + 2): the grouping example
print(expand((x + 3)**2))                   # x**2 + 6*x + 9: the middle term is really there
print(factor(x**2 + 9))                     # x**2 + 9: prime, nothing to factor
print(factor(x**5 - x))                     # x*(x - 1)*(x + 1)*(x**2 + 1)
