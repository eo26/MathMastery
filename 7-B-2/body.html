Chapter 7 · Linear Algebra

Linear Combinations and Span

Give yourself two moves and the freedom to repeat each one, stretch it, or run it backwards. Where can you get to? Usually everywhere. Sometimes only along a line. That question is the heart of linear algebra.

On this page

  • Find the weights that reach a target point.

1Mixing two moves

In 7.B.1 you could do two things with vectors: scale them and add them. Do both at once and you get a linear combination:

c u + d v

Walk u some number of times (c), then walk v some number of times (d). The numbers c and d are the weights (also called coefficients). They can be whole numbers, fractions, or negative (walk backwards). With u = [2, 1], v = [1, 2], c = 2 and d = 1: 2[2, 1] + 1[1, 2] = [4, 2] + [1, 2] = [5, 4].

The faint slanted grid in the explorer is made of copies of u and v. Every crossing point is a combination with whole-number weights, just as the ordinary grid is made of copies of [1, 0] and [0, 1].

2Span: everywhere you can reach

Now let the weights be any numbers. The set of every point you can reach, every cu + dv, is called the span of u and v.

  • If u and v point in different directions, the slanted grid covers the whole plane, and fractional weights fill in the gaps between its lines. The span is the entire plane: every point has exactly one pair of weights.
  • If v is just a multiple of u (they lie on the same line), the grid collapses. Walking v is no different from walking more u. The span is only a line, and any point off it is out of reach, whatever the weights. Try “Squash v onto u’s line” above.
  • If both are the zero vector, you can’t move at all: the span is a single point, the origin.

Vectors like the squashed pair, where one is a combination of the others, are called linearly dependent: one of them is redundant. Vectors that each add a genuinely new direction are linearly independent. (More on both in the Undergraduate tier.)

3When the weights must be whole numbers

A game piece can’t make half a move. Suppose a piece has two jumps, u = [2, 1] and v = [1, 2], each usable forwards or backwards any whole number of times (a bit like a chess knight). Its span is the whole plane, yet it can’t reach every square! Reaching [1, 0] would need c = 2/3 and d = −1/3: fractions of a jump. Only one square in three is reachable.

The same question in one dimension is about coins. With only 5¢ and 7¢ coins, which amounts can you pay exactly? Every amount is a combination 5c + 7d, but now the weights must be whole and not negative (you can’t pay with −2 coins). Small amounts like 3¢ are impossible, yet past a certain point every amount works.

4Now, in symbols

A linear combination of u and v with weights \(c\) and \(d\), entry by entry:

\[ c\,\mathbf{u} + d\,\mathbf{v} = c\begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{bmatrix} + d\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c\,u_1 + d\,v_1 \\ c\,u_2 + d\,v_2 \end{bmatrix} \]
Finding the weights for a target means solving two equations for two unknowns, \(c\) and \(d\).
\[ \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \{\, c\,\mathbf{u} + d\,\mathbf{v} \;:\; c, d \in \mathbb{R} \,\} \]
The span is a set of vectors: all combinations with real weights.
\[ u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \ne 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \mathbb{R}^2 \]
This number is zero exactly when u and v lie on one line. It’s the determinant (7.B.6), and it also measures how much area one cell of the slanted grid covers. For the jumps [2, 1] and [1, 2] it’s 3, which is why only one square in 3 was reachable.

5Where this goes

  • Next (7.B.3): the dot product measures how much two vectors point the same way, which gives a quick way to tell a direction’s share of a combination.
  • Later in this book: a matrix (7.B.4) is a machine that makes linear combinations of its columns. Solving for the weights is solving a system of equations; the slanted grid’s cell area is the determinant (7.B.6); and “which weights?” is undone by the inverse matrix (7.B.7). Span and independence become basis and dimension in the Undergraduate tier.
  • In the world: mixing two paints or two ingredients in different amounts, combining a few basic sound waves into any musical note, describing every color with three primaries, and building any position from steps along chosen directions.

6Words to know

Linear combinationA sum of scaled vectors, like cu + dv.
Weight (coefficient)The scalar multiplying each vector in a combination: c and d.
SpanThe set of all linear combinations of some vectors: everywhere they can reach with any weights.
Linearly dependentAt least one vector is a combination of the others (for two vectors: they lie on one line). It adds nothing new to the span.
Linearly independentNo vector is a combination of the others; each adds a new direction.
LatticeThe grid of points reachable with whole-number weights.

7Check yourself

Four kinds of questions: compute a combination, find the weights for a target, decide whether the span is a point, a line or the plane, and decide whether a point is in the span.
