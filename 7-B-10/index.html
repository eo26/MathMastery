Into 3D: Vectors and 3×3 Matrices

Add one more direction, up, and everything from the flat plane carries over: vectors get a third number, matrices get a third column, and area becomes volume.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Locate points with three coordinates (x, y, z), and read them from flat top, front and side views.
  • Add, scale and measure 3D vectors, using the 3D distance formula.
  • See a 3×3 matrix as a move of all of space: its columns are where î, ĵ and k̂ land.
  • Read the 3×3 determinant as a volume scale factor, and see why a projection (a shadow) has determinant 0.
  • Use the dot product for angles and the cross product for a direction perpendicular to two vectors.

1A third direction

On a map, two numbers pin down a place: how far east (x) and how far north (y). To find a lamp hanging in a room you need a third: how far up (z). A point in space is an ordered triple (x, y, z), measured along three axes that meet at right angles at the origin.

A screen is flat, so we can’t draw space directly. Architects solve this with orthographic views: a top view (looking straight down, you see x and y and lose the height), a front view (looking north, you see x and z) and a side view (looking west, you see y and z). Each view hides one coordinate, but between them they show all three. A fourth, slanted corner view ties them together.

The distance from the origin to (x, y, z) comes from Pythagoras twice. Across the floor to the spot under the point is √(x² + y²). That floor line and the vertical climb z make another right angle, so the full distance is √(x² + y² + z²): the 3D distance formula.

2Vectors in 3D

A 3D vector is a move: so far east, so far north, so far up, written as a column with three components. Everything from the plane carries over, one component at a time. To add vectors, add the east parts, the north parts and the up parts: on a picture, put them head to tail. To scale by k, multiply all three parts by k: 2v is the same trip, twice as far. The length (or magnitude) |v| is the 3D distance formula.

The three unit vectors î = (1, 0, 0), ĵ = (0, 1, 0) and k̂ = (0, 0, 1) point one step along each axis, and every vector is a mix of them: (x, y, z) = xî + yĵ + zk̂.

3A 3×3 matrix moves all of space

In 7.B.4 a 2×2 matrix was a move of the plane, recorded by where î and ĵ land. Space works the same way with one more column. A 3×3 matrix A is a move of all of space, and its three columns are where î, ĵ and k̂ land. Grid lines stay straight and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put, so the unit cube becomes a slanted box called a parallelepiped.

Where does any other vector go? Since v = xî + yĵ + zk̂, the move sends it to Av = x·(column 1) + y·(column 2) + z·(column 3). Same recipe, new landing spots.

4Volume and the determinant

In the plane, the determinant told you how much a move scales area (7.B.6). In space, the 3×3 determinant tells you how much it scales volume: the unit cube (volume 1) becomes a box of volume |det A|, and so does every other shape, by the same factor. The sign carries the same message as before. Negative means space has been turned inside out, like a right glove becoming a left glove in a mirror.

Determinant 0 means the box has no volume at all: space has been squashed flat onto a plane (or a line). The everyday example is a shadow. Sunlight squashes a 3D object onto the flat ground, a move called a projection. Many points in the air share one spot of shadow, so a shadow can’t be undone: you can’t recover a lamp-post’s height from its shadow alone without knowing where the sun is.

5Dot product and cross product

The dot product works exactly as in the plane, with one more term: u·v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ + u₃v₃ = |u||v| cos θ. It measures how much two arrows point the same way. Zero means they are perpendicular (also called orthogonal), so the dot product also finds the angle between two vectors.

Space has something the plane doesn’t: given two arrows, there is a whole new direction sticking out of the flat sheet they span. The cross product u × v is the vector pointing that way. It is perpendicular to both u and v, and its length is the area of the parallelogram they make. Which of the two perpendicular directions? The right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand from u to v, and your thumb points along u × v. Swap the order and the thumb flips: v × u = −(u × v).

63D graphics: turning a model

Every 3D video game and animated film does this millions of times a second. A model is a list of corner points; to turn it, tilt it or resize it, the computer multiplies every corner by one 3×3 matrix. Turning by an angle ψ around the vertical axis, tilting by θ around the east–west axis and resizing by s are each a matrix. Doing one move and then another is the product of their matrices, applied right to left, and just as in 7.B.5, order matters: turn-then-tilt is not tilt-then-turn.

7Traps and non-examples

Compare

Easy to get wrong in 3D

✕ One view isn’t enough

From above, (2, 1, 0) and (2, 1, 3) are the same dot. The front view separates them. A flat view always hides one coordinate.

✕ u × v ≠ v × u

î × ĵ = k̂ points up, but ĵ × î = −k̂ points down. Same length, opposite direction.

✕ A 3×3 determinant isn’t ad − bc

120010302

Using only the top-left 2×2 corner gives 1·1 − 2·0 = 1, but the true determinant is 2: every entry matters. Expand along the top row (section 8), or note that the third column only stretches k̂ by 2.

8Now, in symbols

\[ \mathbf v = \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y\\ z\end{pmatrix} = x\,\hat\imath + y\,\hat\jmath + z\,\hat k, \qquad |\mathbf v| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} \]
Components and length. Adding and scaling work one component at a time.
\[ A\mathbf v = \begin{pmatrix} a & b & c\\ d & e & f\\ g & h & i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x\\ y\\ z\end{pmatrix} = x\begin{pmatrix} a\\ d\\ g\end{pmatrix} + y\begin{pmatrix} b\\ e\\ h\end{pmatrix} + z\begin{pmatrix} c\\ f\\ i\end{pmatrix} \]
The columns are where î, ĵ, k̂ land; Av mixes them with the amounts x, y, z.
\[ \det A = a(ei - fh) - b(di - fg) + c(dh - eg) \]
Expansion along the top row: each top entry times the 2×2 determinant left when you cross out its row and column, with signs + − +. |det A| is the volume scale factor.
\[ \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + u_3v_3 = |\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\cos\theta \]
\[ \mathbf u \times \mathbf v = \begin{pmatrix} u_2v_3 - u_3v_2\\ u_3v_1 - u_1v_3\\ u_1v_2 - u_2v_1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\mathbf u \times \mathbf v| = |\mathbf u||\mathbf v|\sin\theta \]
Perpendicular to both u and v, by the right-hand rule; its length is the parallelogram’s area.
\[ R_z(\psi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0\\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},\quad R_x(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix} \]
Turning about the z-axis is the 2D rotation in the top-left corner; turn-then-tilt is Rx(θ)Rz(ψ).

Checking a cross product and a determinant by hand is tedious, so this is where a computer earns its place:

import numpy as np                       # numerical arrays and linear algebra

u = np.array([2, 1, 0])                  # the vector u
v = np.array([1, -1, 2])                 # the vector v
w = np.cross(u, v)                       # u × v, perpendicular to both
print(w, w @ u, w @ v)                   # [ 2 -4 -3] 0 0: both dot products are 0, so w is perpendicular

A = np.array([[2, 1, 0],                 # columns: where î, ĵ, k̂ land
              [0, 1, 1],
              [1, 0, 1]])
print(np.linalg.det(A))                  # 3.0 (up to rounding): the move triples every volume

t = np.radians(90)                        # 90 degrees, in radians
Rz = np.array([[np.cos(t), -np.sin(t), 0], [np.sin(t), np.cos(t), 0], [0, 0, 1]])   # turn about z
Rx = np.array([[1, 0, 0], [0, np.cos(t), -np.sin(t)], [0, np.sin(t), np.cos(t)]])   # tilt about x
print(np.allclose(Rx @ Rz, Rz @ Rx))      # False: turn-then-tilt differs from tilt-then-turn
