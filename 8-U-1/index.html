Infinite Series

Can you add up infinitely many numbers and get a finite answer? Sometimes. This page is about telling when, and finding the answer when you can.

Basics Undergraduate you are here Masters

  • Sum geometric and telescoping series exactly, and use them for a bouncing ball, repeating decimals and medicine buildup.
  • Use the divergence test, and see why the harmonic series diverges even though its terms shrink to 0.

1Adding forever, one term at a time

Walk halfway to a wall, then half the remaining distance, then half again. The steps are ½, ¼, ⅛, … metres for a wall 1 metre away. Zeno worried that infinitely many steps could never finish. The modern answer is to keep a running total: after n steps you have walked Sn = 1 − 1/2n, which gets as close to 1 as you like. We say the infinite sum is 1.

In general a series is an infinite sum a1 + a2 + a3 + …; each ak is a term. The partial sum Sn = a1 + … + an is the running total after n terms. If the partial sums approach a limit S (in the sense of 8.B.1), the series converges and S is its sum. If they don’t, because they grow forever or keep jumping around, it diverges.

2Series you can sum exactly

A geometric series multiplies by the same ratio r each time: a + ar + ar² + …. Its partial sums have a closed form. Multiply Sn by r and subtract; almost everything cancels: Sn − rSn = a − arn. If |r| < 1, then rn → 0 and

\[ a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1 - r} \quad (|r| < 1), \qquad \text{diverges if } |r| \ge 1. \]

A telescoping series collapses like a folding telescope. Since 1/(k(k + 1)) = 1/k − 1/(k + 1), the partial sum is (1 − ½) + (½ − ⅓) + … + (1/n − 1/(n + 1)) = 1 − 1/(n + 1), so the sum is 1. Geometric series turn up everywhere something repeats with a fixed fraction:

  • A ball dropped from height h that rebounds to a fraction r of its height travels h + 2hr + 2hr² + … = h(1 + r)/(1 − r). It bounces infinitely often, yet stops in finite time: Zeno again.
  • A repeating decimal is a geometric series: 0.333… = 3/10 + 3/100 + … = (3/10)/(1 − 1/10) = 1/3, and 0.999… = (9/10)/(1 − 1/10) = 1, exactly.
  • A medicine taken every few hours, with a fraction f still in the body when the next dose arrives, builds up to D + Df + Df² + … = D/(1 − f): a steady state.

3Shrinking terms aren’t enough

If a series converges, its terms must shrink to 0, because an = Sn − Sn₋₁ and both partial sums approach the same S. Turned around, this is the divergence test: if the terms don’t go to 0, the series diverges. So 1/2 + 2/3 + 3/4 + … diverges (terms → 1), and so does 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + … (terms never settle).

The converse is false, and the harmonic series 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ + … is the classic proof. Group its terms: ½, then ⅓ + ¼ (both at least ¼, so at least ½), then ⅕ through ⅛ (four terms, each at least ⅛: at least ½ again), and so on. You get infinitely many halves, so the sum is infinite. It just gets there very slowly: Sn ≈ ln n + 0.577. It takes 12,367 terms to pass 10.

You can feel that slowness with a stack of identical books. Push the top book out by ½ of a book length, the next by ¼, then ⅙, …, 1/(2k). Each overhang is the most you can push before the books above topple, and the total overhang is half the harmonic series. With enough books the top one sticks out as far as you like, but you need 4 books for one full length, 31 for two, 227 for three and 1,674 for four.

4Tests for series with positive terms

Most series can’t be summed exactly, but we can still decide whether they converge. For positive terms the partial sums only go up, so they either level off or run away. Three standard tests:

  • Comparison test. If 0 ≤ ak ≤ bk and Σbk converges, so does Σak; if Σak diverges, so does Σbk. Example: 1/(k² + 3) < 1/k², so it converges; 1/√k ≥ 1/k, so it diverges. The limit comparison version only asks that ak/bk approach a positive number.
  • Integral test. If ak = f(k) for a positive decreasing f, the series and ∫₁∞ f(x) dx converge or diverge together, because the rectangles of the sum fit just above and just below the curve (picture below). It gives the p-series rule: Σ 1/kp converges exactly when p > 1.
  • Ratio test. If ak+1/ak → L: the series converges if L < 1 and diverges if L > 1, because far out it behaves like a geometric series with ratio L. If L = 1 the test says nothing. It is the test to use for factorials and powers, like 3k/k! or k²/2k.

5Alternating series and the danger of reordering

An alternating series flips sign every term, like 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ¼ + …. The alternating series test: if the sizes |ak| shrink steadily to 0, the series converges. The partial sums zigzag, each step shorter than the last, closing in from both sides. A bonus: the error after n terms is less than the next term. The alternating harmonic series converges to ln 2 ≈ 0.693.

Notice something odd: the alternating harmonic series converges, but the series of its sizes (the harmonic series) doesn’t. That is called conditional convergence. When Σ|ak| also converges, the series converges absolutely, and then it behaves like an ordinary finite sum: any order gives the same answer.

Conditional convergence is fragile. The positive terms alone add to infinity, and so do the negative ones, while the sum is held to ln 2 only by the way they take turns. Riemann’s rearrangement theorem says you can reorder the terms to reach any target you like: add positive terms until you pass it, then negative terms until you drop below, and repeat. Every term gets used exactly once, and because the terms shrink, the overshoots shrink too.

6Traps and non-examples

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ “The terms go to 0, so it converges”

Harmonic series: the terms (orange bars) shrink to 0, but the partial sums (blue) keep climbing. The divergence test can only prove divergence, never convergence.

✕ Regrouping a divergent series

1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + …: grouped as (1 − 1) + (1 − 1) + … it looks like 0, grouped as 1 + (−1 + 1) + … like 1. The partial sums jump between 1 and 0 forever, so there is no sum to argue about.

✕ Reading ratio = 1 as a verdict

ak+1/ak → 1 for both 1/k (diverges) and 1/k² (converges). When the ratio test gives 1, it tells you nothing: switch tests.

7Now, in symbols

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k = \lim_{n\to\infty} S_n, \qquad S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k \]
\[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} ar^k = \frac{a}{1-r}\ (|r|<1), \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k(k+1)} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(1 - \frac{1}{n+1}\Big) = 1 \]
\[ \sum a_k \text{ converges} \Rightarrow a_k \to 0, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^p} \text{ converges} \iff p > 1, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1k = \ln n + \gamma + o(1),\ \gamma \approx 0.5772 \]
\[ \int_1^{\infty} f \le \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} f(k) \le f(1) + \int_1^{\infty} f, \qquad \lim_{k\to\infty}\Big|\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\Big| = L < 1 \Rightarrow \text{converges} \]
\[ \Big|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k+1}b_k - S_n\Big| \le b_{n+1} \quad (b_k \downarrow 0), \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k} = \ln 2 \]

The slow growth of the harmonic series and the alternating-series error bound are both easy to see by brute force:

import math                                         # logarithms and the constant pi

H = 0.0                                             # running total of the harmonic series
for n in range(1, 1_000_001):                       # add the first million terms
    H += 1 / n                                      # the n-th term is 1/n
print(H, math.log(1_000_000) + 0.5772)              # 14.39 vs 14.39: grows like ln n + γ, without bound

A = sum((-1) ** (k + 1) / k for k in range(1, 101))  # first 100 terms of 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − …
print(abs(A - math.log(2)), 1 / 101)                # error 0.00496 is below the next term, 1/101 ≈ 0.0099

9Words to know

SeriesAn infinite sum a1 + a2 + a3 + ….
TermOne of the numbers ak being added.
Partial sumSn: the total of the first n terms.
ConvergeThe partial sums approach a finite limit.
DivergeThe partial sums don’t approach a finite limit: they grow without bound or keep oscillating.
Sum of a seriesThe limit of its partial sums, when it exists.
Geometric seriesa + ar + ar² + …; sums to a/(1 − r) when |r| < 1.
Ratio (r)The fixed multiplier from one term of a geometric series to the next.
Telescoping seriesA series whose partial sums collapse because neighbouring pieces cancel.
Repeating decimalA decimal like 0.142857142857…; it is a geometric series, so always a fraction.
Steady stateThe level a repeated, partly decaying input settles to: D/(1 − f).
Divergence testIf ak does not go to 0, the series diverges.
Harmonic series1 + ½ + ⅓ + …; diverges, growing like ln n.
p-seriesΣ 1/kp; converges exactly when p > 1.
Comparison testA positive series below a convergent one converges; above a divergent one diverges.
Integral testFor positive decreasing f, Σf(k) and ∫f converge or diverge together.
Ratio testIf |ak+1/ak| → L: converges if L < 1, diverges if L > 1, no verdict if L = 1.
Alternating seriesA series whose terms switch sign each time.
Alternating series testIf the sizes decrease to 0, an alternating series converges, with error below the next term.
Absolute convergenceΣ|ak| converges; the sum then doesn’t depend on order.
Conditional convergenceΣak converges but Σ|ak| diverges.
Rearrangement theoremRiemann: a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to sum to any number, or to diverge.
