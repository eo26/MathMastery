Chapter 7 · Linear Algebra

Independence, Basis and Dimension: A First Look

Some sets of arrows reach everywhere, some only a line or a sheet, and some carry passengers that add nothing new. Counting the truly different directions is what “dimension” means.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Build linear combinations and see the span of a set of vectors: a point, a line, a plane or all of space.
  • Tell linearly independent vectors from dependent ones, where one is a mix of the others.
  • Use a basis to give every point its coordinates, and see that coordinates depend on the basis.
  • Count dimension, and read the rank of a matrix as its number of independent columns.

1Mixing arrows: span

Give someone a few arrows and two permissions: stretch any arrow by any amount (including backwards, or to zero), and add the results. What they build is a linear combination: c₁v₁ + c₂v₂ + c₃v₃. Everything they could ever build is the span of the arrows.

One nonzero arrow reaches a whole line through the origin: all its stretches. Two arrows pointing in different directions reach the whole plane. But two arrows along the same line still only reach that line: the second one is just a stretch of the first, so it adds nothing new.

That is the key idea. A vector is redundant if it is already in the span of the others, that is, if it is a mix of them. A set with a redundant vector is called linearly dependent. A set where every vector adds a genuinely new direction is linearly independent.

2Basis and coordinates

A basis is a set of vectors that does two jobs at once: it reaches everything (its span is the whole space) and it carries no passengers (it is independent). Then every point can be reached in exactly one way, and the amounts are the point’s coordinates in that basis.

Our usual coordinates are just coordinates in the basis î, ĵ. Pick a different basis and the same point gets different numbers. A city whose streets run at an angle works this way: “3 blocks along Main Street, then 1 block along Oak Avenue” is a perfectly good address, measured in the street grid instead of north and east. In the basis-grid mode above, the slanted grid lines are the streets.

Why exactly one way? If a point could be reached two different ways, subtracting them would give a mix of the basis vectors equal to zero with amounts not all zero, and then one basis vector could be written using the others: a redundant passenger. Independence rules that out.

3Dimension and rank

Every basis of the plane has exactly 2 vectors; every basis of space has exactly 3. Fewer can’t reach everything, and more must include a redundant one. That count is the dimension: a line has dimension 1, a plane 2, space 3. A single point (just the origin) has dimension 0.

For a matrix, the dimension of the span of its columns is called its rank: the number of truly independent columns. This ties together the chapter. A 3 × 3 matrix sends î, ĵ, k̂ to its columns, so its output is exactly the span of the columns. Rank 3: the output is all of space, the volume scale is not zero, det ≠ 0, and the move can be undone. Rank 2 or less: space is flattened onto a plane or a line, det = 0, and some systems Ax = b have no solution (7.B.9). Row reduction counts the rank for you: it is the number of pivots.

4In the kitchen and on a screen

A food can be described by a vector of nutrients: (protein, carbs, fat) per serving. A meal is a linear combination: so many servings of each. Which nutrient targets can you hit exactly with what’s in the pantry? Exactly the span of the food vectors. A snack bar made from 2 servings of oats and 1 of peanut butter is a redundant food: anything it can do, oats and peanut butter already do.

Every colour on a screen is a mix of red, green and blue light, three independent vectors: a basis for colour. Any other three independent lights would also be a basis (the coordinates change, and some colours would need a negative amount of light, which a real lamp can’t give). But red, green and yellow are dependent, since yellow light is red plus green, so they only reach colours with no blue at all: a plane, not the whole space.

5Traps and non-examples

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ No two parallel ≠ independent

(1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0) and (1, 1, 0): no two are parallel, yet the third is the sum of the first two. All three lie in the floor plane. Dependence can involve the whole set.

✕ More vectors ≠ more dimensions

Four arrows in the plane can never be independent: the plane only has room for two directions. Any third arrow is a mix of two independent ones.

✕ The zero vector is never independent

0 = 0·v₁ is a mix of anything. Any set containing the zero vector is dependent: it adds no direction at all.

6Now, in symbols

\[ \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v_1,\dots,\mathbf v_k\} = \{\, c_1\mathbf v_1 + \dots + c_k\mathbf v_k \;:\; c_1,\dots,c_k \in \mathbb R \,\} \]
Everything you can build by stretching and adding.
\[ \text{independent} \iff \Big( c_1\mathbf v_1 + \dots + c_k\mathbf v_k = \mathbf 0 \;\Rightarrow\; c_1 = \dots = c_k = 0 \Big) \]
The only way to mix them into zero is to use none of each. Otherwise one of them is a mix of the rest.
\[ \mathbf p = a\,\mathbf b_1 + b\,\mathbf b_2 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \begin{pmatrix} | & | \\ \mathbf b_1 & \mathbf b_2 \\ | & | \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf p \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b\end{pmatrix} = B^{-1}\mathbf p \]
Coordinates in a basis solve a system whose columns are the basis vectors; a basis makes B invertible (7.B.7).
\[ \operatorname{rank} A = \dim \operatorname{span}(\text{columns of } A) = \text{number of pivots}, \qquad n\times n:\ \operatorname{rank} A = n \iff \det A \ne 0 \]

Counting pivots by hand is fine for 3 × 3; for real data a computer does it:

import numpy as np                           # numerical arrays and linear algebra

oats, milk, pb = [5, 27, 3], [8, 12, 8], [8, 6, 16]   # (protein, carbs, fat) per serving
bar = [18, 60, 22]                           # a snack bar: 2 oats + 1 peanut butter
F = np.array([oats, milk, pb, bar]).T        # foods as columns: a 3 × 4 matrix
print(np.linalg.matrix_rank(F))              # 3: four foods, but only three independent directions
print(np.linalg.matrix_rank(F[:, [0, 2, 3]])) # 2: oats, peanut butter and the bar span only a plane

B = np.array([[2, -1], [1, 1]])              # basis vectors as columns: Main St (2, 1), Oak Ave (−1, 1)
print(np.linalg.solve(B, [5, 4]))            # [3. 1.]: the point (5, 4) is 3 blocks along Main, 1 along Oak

8Words to know

Linear combinationA mix c₁v₁ + … + ckvk: stretch each vector, then add.
SpanThe set of all linear combinations of some vectors: everything they can reach.
Redundant vectorOne that is already a mix of the others, so it adds nothing to the span.
Linearly dependentSome vector in the set is a mix of the others (equivalently, a mix with not-all-zero amounts gives 0).
Linearly independentNo vector is a mix of the others; each adds a new direction.
BasisAn independent set whose span is the whole space. Every point is reached in exactly one way.
Coordinates (in a basis)The amounts of each basis vector needed to reach a point.
Standard basisî, ĵ (and k̂): the basis behind ordinary coordinates.
DimensionThe number of vectors in any basis: 1 for a line, 2 for a plane, 3 for space.
RankThe number of independent columns of a matrix: the dimension of their span. Also the number of pivots.
Full rankA square matrix whose columns are independent: rank n, det ≠ 0, invertible.
