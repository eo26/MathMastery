Chapter 1 · Numbers & Arithmetic

Counting and the Number Line

A number answers two questions at once: how many? and where? Once you see both, adding stops being a calculation and becomes a movement.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • See a number as both a count and a position.
  • See adding as sliding the whole number line, not just one number.
  • Read negative numbers as places left of 0, and as amounts owed.
  • Work backwards: given a before and an after, find the slide.

1How many?

The oldest piece of mathematics we have is a bone with notches cut into it, roughly 20,000 years old. One notch per thing. That is all counting is: matching each thing to one mark. Five things, five marks, and we call that amount 5.

In this book a counted thing is a dot: ●. Three dots is 3. No dots at all is also an answer to “how many?”, and its name is 0. Zero took humanity a surprisingly long time to accept as a number, but it is the most important one on this page: it is where everything starts.

2A count is also a place

Now lay the dots in a row, each one taking up exactly one step of space, starting from a mark labeled 0. The end of the row lands on a tick mark. That tick is the number. “3” now means two things at once: three dots, and the place three steps to the right of 0.

This is the number line: every number gets an address, and its distance from 0 is how many.

What is to the left of 0? Something less than nothing. That sounds impossible until you think about owing. If you borrow 2 cookies from a friend, then until you pay them back you have less than nothing: you owe 2. We draw an owed thing as a hollow dot ○ and lay owed dots out to the left. Owing 2 is the place two steps left of 0, written −2.

The rule that makes this work: a dot you have and a dot you owe cancel. Have 1, owe 1, and you are back at 0.

3Adding is sliding

Here is the shift in thinking that the rest of this book depends on. Read 2 + 3 not as “combine a pile of 2 with a pile of 3,” but as an instruction: slide by 3.

And a slide doesn’t move just the point at 2. It moves the whole line. Every point slides 3 to the right at the same moment: 2 lands on 5, 0 lands on 3, −4 lands on −1. That’s why the explorer below has two lines. The moving line carries its original labels with it; the fixed ruler underneath never moves, so you can read where everything ended up.

Play with every preset, then look for these five things. Each one comes back, under a grander name, much later in the book.

  1. Every point moves the same distance. The spacing between ticks never changes. The line slides; it doesn’t stretch. (Stretching is the next page.)
  2. 0 moves too. Adding drags the starting mark along with everything else. Hold on to this; it’s exactly what multiplication will not do.
  3. A slide of 0 does nothing. Every point stays put. Zero is the “do-nothing” slide.
  4. Every slide can be undone. Slide +5, then −5, and you’re back where you began. +5 and −5 are opposites.
  5. Order doesn’t matter. Slide 2 then 3, or 3 then 2: you land in the same place. Both are a slide of 5.

4Subtracting is sliding left

5 − 2 is the same move as 5 + (−2): a slide of 2 to the left. So subtraction isn’t a second operation to learn. It’s adding a leftward slide. In the explorer, each hop left along the ruler crosses out one of the dots you have; once you run out, new owed dots start to pile up on the left of 0.

Subtraction also answers a second, very useful question: how far apart are two places? If a point started at 2 and ended at 5, the slide was 5 − 2 = 3. That’s the skill the quiz at the bottom tests.

5Now, in symbols

The formulas come last, because by now they only write down what you’ve already watched happen. Look back at the explorer: the purple starting point is labeled a, the hops along the ruler are labeled s, and under the ruler you’ll find a where the point started and b where it landed. Those three labels are the whole formula:

\[ a + s = b \]
Start at \(a\) (the purple point), slide by \(s\) (the hops), land on \(b\) (the ruler). With the first preset: \(2 + 3 = 5\).

The picture said more than that, though: the whole line moves, not just \(a\). The second point you can track by tapping is any other point; call it \(x\). Mathematicians write the slide as a rule for every point at once:

\[ x \;\longmapsto\; x + s \qquad \text{for every point } x \]
Read the arrow as “goes to”: every point goes to itself plus the slide.

And working backwards, which is exactly what the quiz asks you to do: if you can see \(a\) and \(b\), the slide is their difference.

\[ s = b - a \]
“How far did it move?” is a subtraction.

6Where this goes

  • Next (1.B.2): multiplication is also a movement of the whole line, but it stretches the line around 0, and 0 stays put. Adding is the one move that carries 0 along with everything else.
  • Later in this book: on a flat page, a slide can go in any direction, not just left or right. A slide like that is drawn as an arrow called a vector, and adding vectors means doing one slide and then another, exactly like here. And the five things you noticed in the explorer turn out to be the rules of a structure mathematicians call a group, which shows up all over higher mathematics.
  • In the world: the number line is everywhere once you look. Elevator floors below the ground floor, temperatures below zero, heights below sea level, and years on a timeline all work like positions left and right of 0, and every change in them is a slide.

7Words to know

Natural numbersThe counting numbers 1, 2, 3, …
Whole numbersThe natural numbers plus 0.
IntegersThe whole numbers and their negatives: …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …
Number lineA line where every number has a place, and distance from 0 is size.
OriginThe point 0, where measuring starts.
Positive / negativeRight of 0 (have) / left of 0 (owe).
OppositeThe same distance from 0 on the other side: 5 and −5. They cancel.
TranslationThe formal name for a slide: every point moves the same distance the same way.
