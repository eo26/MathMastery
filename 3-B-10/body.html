Radicals and Rational Exponents

A square root answers “what side gives this area?”, and a cube root “what edge gives this volume?”. Written as exponents, roots become fractions, and every exponent rule you already know keeps working.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Read square roots, cube roots and nth roots as undoing powers, and know when a root is a real number.
  • Simplify radicals such as √72 = 6√2, and rationalize a denominator.
  • Use rational exponents: x1/n = ⁿ√x and xm/n = (ⁿ√x)m, with all the exponent rules.

  • See square-root and fractional-power laws in pendulums, horizons, animals, paper and music.

1Roots undo powers

A square of area 25 has side 5, because 5² = 25. That side is the square root: √25 = 5. A cube of volume 8 has edge 2, because 2³ = 8: the cube root ∛8 = 2. In general the nth root ⁿ√x is the number whose nth power is x. The whole expression is a radical; the number inside is the radicand, and the small n is the index (an index of 2 isn’t written).

Numbers like 25 and 49 are perfect squares (and 8, 27 are perfect cubes), with whole-number roots. Most roots aren’t whole: √20 is between 4 and 5, because 20 is between 16 and 25, and its decimal goes on forever without repeating. It is irrational.

Two subtleties. First, (−5)² = 25 as well, so 25 has two square roots, 5 and −5. The symbol √ always means the principal root, the one that isn’t negative: √25 = 5, and the solutions of x² = 25 are ±√25 = ±5. Second, no real number squares to a negative, so √(−4) is not a real number (3.B.15 builds new numbers for it). Odd roots don’t have this problem: (−3)³ = −27, so ∛(−27) = −3.

2Simplifying radicals

Roots split over multiplication and division: √(ab) = √a · √b and √(a/b) = √a/√b (for a, b ≥ 0). So pull out the biggest perfect square you can find: √72 = √(36 · 2) = √36 · √2 = 6√2. The picture is a square of area 72 cut into a 6 × 6 grid of small squares, each of area 2: its side is six small sides, 6√2. A radical is in simplest form when the radicand has no perfect-square factor left.

Roots do not split over addition: √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5, not 3 + 4. Like terms work as with x: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2, but √2 + √3 doesn’t combine.

Fractions with a root on the bottom, like 6/√3, are usually rewritten with a whole-number denominator: multiply top and bottom by √3 to get 6√3/3 = 2√3. That’s rationalizing the denominator. It doesn’t change the value, only the form, and before calculators it turned a hard division (6 ÷ 1.732…) into an easy one.

3Roots are fractional exponents

What could 91/2 mean? If the exponent rules from 3.B.7 are to keep working, then 91/2 · 91/2 = 91/2 + 1/2 = 9¹ = 9. A number times itself giving 9 is 3, so 91/2 = √9 = 3. In the same way 81/3 = ∛8 = 2, and in general

\[ x^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{x}, \qquad x^{m/n} = \left(\sqrt[n]{x}\right)^m = \sqrt[n]{x^m}, \qquad x^{-m/n} = \frac{1}{x^{m/n}}. \]

Read 82/3 as “take the cube root, then square”: ∛8 = 2, 2² = 4. Taking the root first keeps the numbers small. These rational exponents fill the gaps in the ladder of powers: on the way from 4⁰ = 1 to 4¹ = 4, the halfway exponent 41/2 = 2 is where you are after multiplying by 2 once, because two equal steps that multiply to 4 must each be ×2.

4Radical equations and fake solutions

To solve √(x + 3) = x − 3, get rid of the root by squaring both sides: x + 3 = (x − 3)², a quadratic. It gives x = 1 or x = 6. But check them: at x = 6, √9 = 3 = 6 − 3 ✓; at x = 1, √4 = 2 but 1 − 3 = −2 ✗.

The fake answer x = 1 is an extraneous solution. Squaring can’t tell 2 from −2, so the squared equation also solves −√(x + 3) = x − 3. On the graph, squaring adds the mirror-image lower branch −√(x + 3), and the extra crossing is there. The rule: after squaring, always check every answer in the original equation.

    5Roots in the world

    Whenever one quantity grows like the square of another, the other grows like the square root of the first, and many natural laws are fractional powers. Quadruple a pendulum’s length and its swing takes only twice as long. Climb four times higher and you see only twice as far. Animals’ energy use grows like mass3/4, so small animals burn energy fastest. And a sheet of A4 paper and a piano both rely on roots of 2.

    6Traps and non-examples

    Compare

    Easy to get wrong

    ✕ √(9 + 16) = √9 + √16

    The right triangle with legs 3 and 4 has hypotenuse √(9 + 16) = 5. Walking 3 then 4 is 7, which is longer. Roots split over × and ÷, never over + and −.

    ✕ √(x²) = x

    Try x = −3: √9 = 3, not −3. The principal root is never negative, so √(x²) = |x|, the V-shaped graph. (Odd roots are fine: ∛(x³) = x.)

    ✕ 16−1/2 = −4

    A negative exponent means “one over”, not “negative”: 16−1/2 = 1/√16 = ¼. And 82/3 is (∛8)² = 4, not 8 × 2/3.

    7Now, in symbols

    \[ \sqrt[n]{x} = y \iff y^n = x \quad (y \ge 0 \text{ when } n \text{ is even}), \qquad \sqrt{x^2} = |x|, \qquad \sqrt[3]{x^3} = x \]
    \[ \sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}, \qquad \sqrt{\tfrac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt a}{\sqrt b} \quad (a, b \ge 0), \qquad \frac{a}{\sqrt b} = \frac{a\sqrt b}{b} \]
    \[ x^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{x}, \qquad x^{m/n} = \big(\sqrt[n]{x}\big)^m = \sqrt[n]{x^m}, \qquad x^{-m/n} = \frac{1}{x^{m/n}} \]
    \[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}, \qquad d \approx 3.57\sqrt{h}, \qquad B \propto M^{3/4}, \qquad \text{A-paper } \tfrac{\text{long}}{\text{short}} = \sqrt2, \qquad \text{semitone} = 2^{1/12} \]

    How do you find √2 without a calculator? Guess, then average the guess with 2 ÷ guess: if the guess is too big, 2 ÷ guess is too small, so the average is better. This 4,000-year-old Babylonian method doubles the number of correct digits each step:

    x = 1.0                        # first guess for √2
for step in range(5):          # five improvements
    x = (x + 2 / x) / 2        # average the guess with 2 ÷ guess
    print(step + 1, x)         # 1.5, 1.4166…, 1.41421568…, 1.41421356237469…, then no change
print(x * x)                   # 2.0000000000000004: squaring undoes the root (to rounding)

    9Words to know

    Square root√x: the non-negative number whose square is x; the side of a square of area x.
    Cube root∛x: the number whose cube is x; the edge of a cube of volume x.
    nth rootⁿ√x: the number whose nth power is x.
    RadicalAn expression with a root sign, such as 3√5.
    RadicandThe number under the root sign.
    IndexThe small n in ⁿ√x; 2 when not written.
    Principal rootThe non-negative root that √ (or any even root) means.
    Perfect square / cubeA whole number with a whole-number square / cube root: 49, 64.
    Irrational numberA number that isn’t a fraction, such as √2; its decimal never ends or repeats.
    Simplest formA radical whose radicand has no perfect-square (or cube…) factor left.
    Rationalizing the denominatorRewriting a fraction so no root is left on the bottom.
    Rational exponentA fractional exponent: xm/n = (ⁿ√x)m.
    Radical equationAn equation with the unknown under a root, such as √(x + 3) = x − 3.
    Extraneous solutionA value produced by the solving steps (such as squaring) that fails the original equation.
