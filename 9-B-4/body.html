Chapter 9 · Probability

Conditional Probability

New information changes the odds. “Given that A happened” throws away every outcome where A didn’t, and the chance of B is recounted inside what’s left.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Find a conditional probability P(B | A), read “B given A”, by shrinking the sample space to the reduced sample space A.
  • Read conditional probabilities from a two-way table, and handle draws with and without replacement.
  • Use a tree diagram: multiply along each path, then add the paths you want.
  • Tell P(B | A) from P(A | B), and see why a positive result on a good test can still mean you probably don’t have a rare condition.

1Being told something changes the chance

Two dice are rolled behind a screen. The chance the first die shows 6 is 1/6. Now a friend peeks and says: “the total is 10 or more.” Only six pairs give a total that high: (4,6), (5,5), (6,4), (5,6), (6,5), (6,6). Every other outcome is ruled out. Of the six that remain, three have a 6 on the first die, so the chance is now 3/6 = ½.

That’s a conditional probability. We write it P(B | A) and read it “the probability of B given A”: the chance of B once we know A happened. The bar “|” means “given”. Knowing A shrinks the sample space to just the outcomes in A, the reduced sample space, and B is recounted inside it:

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{\text{outcomes in both A and B}}{\text{outcomes in A}} = \frac{|A \text{ and } B|}{|A|}. \]

In the explorer, A (what we’re told) is blue and B is yellow, as in 9.B.3, and outcomes in both are green. Choose “P(B | A)” and everything outside A is crossed out. Press Play to watch the recount.

2From counts to a formula

Divide the top and bottom of the count by the total number of outcomes and you get probabilities instead of counts:

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(A)}. \]

In a two-way table, “given A” means: use only A’s row. The row total is the new denominator. In the class survey, 12 students play music and 7 of them also play a sport, so P(sport | music) = 7/12 ≈ 0.58. Compare P(sport) = 18/30 = 0.6 for the whole class: knowing a student plays music barely changes the chance, which fits what 9.B.3 found: sport and music are nearly independent. In fact independence means exactly P(B | A) = P(B): being told A doesn’t change the chance of B.

3Without replacement

Draw two marbles from a bag of 3 red and 2 white. If you put the first one back before drawing again, that’s drawing with replacement: the second draw is a fresh start, still 3/5 red. Usually you keep it, which is drawing without replacement, and then the second draw depends on the first. After a red, the bag holds 2 red and 2 white, so P(second red | first red) = 2/4 = ½. Try the “Two marbles” example above: the diagonal is crossed out because you can’t draw the same marble twice, leaving 5 × 4 = 20 equally likely ordered pairs.

Cards work the same way. P(first card is an ace) = 4/52; given that, only 3 aces are left among 51 cards, so P(second ace | first ace) = 3/51.

4Tree diagrams

Rearranging the formula gives the general multiplication rule, which works whether or not the events are independent:

\[ P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A). \]

A tree diagram organises this. The first set of branches splits on A or not A, and each branch then splits on B or not B, labelled with the chance of B given the branch you’re on. Follow a path from the root to a leaf and multiply the numbers along it: that’s the chance of that whole story. The paths don’t overlap, so to find the chance of B overall you add every path that ends in B. Adding up the cases like this is called the law of total probability. 5P(A | B) is not P(B | A)

Most people who play professional basketball are tall, but most tall people don’t play professional basketball. P(tall | pro player) is high; P(pro player | tall) is tiny. Swapping what’s given changes the denominator, and that changes everything.

This matters most for tests. Suppose 1% of people have a condition. A screening test catches it 90% of the time, but also comes back positive for 9% of people who don’t have it: a false positive. (A false negative is the opposite: a negative result for someone who does have it.) You test positive. What’s the chance you have the condition? Not 90%. Picture 1,000 people, which is called using natural frequencies: 10 have the condition and 9 of them test positive; 990 don’t, and about 89 of them test positive anyway. Of the 98 positives, only 9 have the condition, about 9%. The test is good, but the condition is rare: its base rate is low, so the false positives from the huge healthy group swamp the true ones. Choose the “Medical test” example and “P(A | B)” above to see it in dots.

A simulation of a million people agrees:

import random                                      # Python's random-number tools
people = 1_000_000                                 # simulate a million people
positive = 0                                       # how many test positive
sick_and_positive = 0                              # how many test positive AND have the condition
for _ in range(people):                            # go through the people one at a time
    has_it = random.random() < 0.01                # 1% have the condition
    if has_it:                                     # someone who has it...
        test = random.random() < 0.90              # ...tests positive 90% of the time
    else:                                          # someone who doesn't have it...
        test = random.random() < 0.09              # ...still tests positive 9% of the time
    positive += test                               # True counts as 1, False as 0
    sick_and_positive += has_it and test           # positives who really have it
print(sick_and_positive / positive)                # P(has it | positive): about 0.09, not 0.9

This is why a positive screening result is usually followed by a second, different test. Turning P(B | A) into P(A | B) like this is Bayes’ theorem, which 9.U develops in full.

6Traps

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ Flipping the condition

P(positive | condition) = 90% describes the test. P(condition | positive) = 9% describes you after a positive result. Different denominators: the 10 people with the condition versus the 98 positives.

✕ Dividing by the wrong total

P(sport | music) uses only the 12 music students: 7/12. Dividing by the whole class, 7/30, gives P(sport and music) instead.

✕ Forgetting the first draw

Two cards: P(both aces) is not 4/52 × 4/52. After one ace is gone, 3 aces are left among 51 cards: 4/52 × 3/51 = 1/221.

7Now, in symbols

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \quad (P(A) > 0), \qquad \text{equally likely outcomes: } P(B \mid A) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A|} \]
\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B) \]
\[ P(B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) + P(A^{c})\,P(B \mid A^{c}) \qquad \text{(law of total probability: add the paths)} \]
\[ A, B \text{ independent} \iff P(B \mid A) = P(B), \qquad P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A)\,P(B \mid A)}{P(B)} \]

The last formula is Bayes’ theorem: it is just “path to A and B” ÷ “all paths to B”, the calculation the tree does when you choose P(A | B).

9Words to know

Conditional probabilityThe chance of an event once you know another happened.
Given“We know this happened”. Written with a bar: P(B | A).
P(B | A)The probability of B given A: |A and B| ÷ |A|.
Reduced sample spaceWhat’s left of the sample space once the given information rules outcomes out.
With replacementEach item is put back before the next draw: draws don’t affect each other.
Without replacementItems are kept: later draws depend on earlier ones.
Tree diagramBranches for each stage, labelled with conditional probabilities.
Branch / pathOne split of the tree / a route from the root to a leaf.
Multiplication ruleP(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A): multiply along a path.
Law of total probabilityAdd the paths: P(B) = P(A)P(B | A) + P(not A)P(B | not A).
IndependentP(B | A) = P(B): knowing A doesn’t change the chance of B.
False positive / false negativeA test says yes when the truth is no / says no when the truth is yes.
Base rateHow common something is before any test or evidence: P(A).
Natural frequenciesCounts out of a round number (“9 of 98 people”) instead of probabilities.
