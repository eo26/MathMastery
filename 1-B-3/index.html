Chapter 1 · Numbers & Arithmetic

Fractions, Decimals, and the Gaps Between Them

The whole numbers leave gaps on the number line. Cut those gaps into equal pieces and you get fractions; keep zooming in by tens and you get decimals. And no matter how far you zoom, there is always another number in between.

On this page

  • See a fraction a/b as a point: cut each whole into b equal pieces and count a of them.
  • See a decimal as an address found by zooming in by tens, and why 1/3 = 0.333… never ends.

  • Add fractions by cutting into same-size pieces, and multiply them as parts of parts.

1Between the whole numbers

On the number line from 1.B.1, the whole numbers sit one step apart, with empty-looking gaps between them. But real things land in those gaps all the time: half a pizza, three quarters of an hour, a board 2½ feet long. To name those places, cut each gap into equal pieces.

Cut the space from 0 to 1 into b equal pieces, then count a of them from 0. Where you stop is the fraction ab. The bottom number, the denominator, says how many pieces each whole is cut into (it names the piece size). The top number, the numerator, says how many of those pieces you take. A fraction is a point on the number line, just like 3 or −2.

A fraction can be bigger than 1: 73 means seven thirds, which runs past 2. Written as a mixed number that’s 2⅓: two wholes and one more third.

Try ½, then type 2 and 4, then 3 and 6. They all land on the very same point. Fractions that name the same point are called equivalent fractions: cutting into twice as many pieces and taking twice as many changes the name, not the place. (That’s the same idea as equivalent ratios in 1.B.4.)

2Decimals: an address by zooming

Fractions can use any piece size. Decimals always cut into tens. To find a point’s decimal, cut the gap it’s in into 10 pieces and note which piece it lands in: that’s the first digit after the decimal point (tenths). Then zoom into that piece, cut it into 10 again, and note the next digit (hundredths). Keep going.

For ¾, the first zoom lands in piece 7, the next in piece 5, and then the point sits exactly on a cut: 0.75, done. That’s a terminating decimal. But for ⅓, every zoom lands in piece 3, forever: 0.333…, a repeating decimal, written 0.3. The digits never stop, yet the point is perfectly definite: exactly one third of the way from 0 to 1.

3There is always another one in between

Pick any two different fractions, as close together as you like. Is there a fraction between them? Always. Add them and divide by 2: their average sits exactly halfway between. And then there’s another between that one and either end, and another, forever. Between any two fractions, there are infinitely many more. The number line has no empty gaps that fractions can’t reach into.

And yet, surprisingly, the fractions still don’t fill the line completely. Some points, like the length of a square’s diagonal (√2), are not any fraction at all. That puzzle is waiting in 1.B.9.

4Adding fractions: make the pieces the same size

Adding is sliding (1.B.1), so ½ + ⅓ means: start at ½, slide by ⅓. But to count the result, the pieces must be the same size. Halves and thirds don’t match, so re-cut both into a size that fits each evenly: sixths. Then ½ = 3/6 and ⅓ = 2/6, and 3 sixths plus 2 sixths is 5/6. A shared piece size is a common denominator.

The adding trap: adding tops and bottoms, ½ + ⅓ = 2/5, can’t be right. 2/5 is smaller than ½, and you added something positive to ½!

5Multiplying fractions: a part of a part

In 1.B.2, multiplying by ½ squeezed every point halfway toward 0. So ½ × ¾ means “half of three quarters.” Picture it as an area: a square of side 1, cut into quarters one way with 3 shaded, then cut in half the other way with 1 shaded. The overlap is 3 pieces out of 8: 3/8. Multiplying fractions is the one easy case: top times top, bottom times bottom.

Notice that multiplying two fractions smaller than 1 gives something smaller than either. A part of a part is a smaller part.

6Now, in symbols

In the fraction maker, b was the number of pieces per whole and a the number of pieces taken:

\[ \frac{a}{b} \;=\; a \times \frac{1}{b}, \qquad \frac{a}{b} = \frac{k\,a}{k\,b} \;\;(k \ne 0) \]
\(a\) hops of size \(\tfrac{1}{b}\). Multiplying top and bottom by the same \(k\) gives an equivalent fraction: same point.

Adding needs a common denominator; multiplying doesn’t:

\[ \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a\,d + c\,b}{b\,d}, \qquad \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a\,c}{b\,d} \]
\(\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac{3 + 2}{6} = \tfrac56\) and \(\tfrac34 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac38\).

And between any two numbers \(p < q\) sits their average:

\[ p \;<\; \frac{p + q}{2} \;<\; q \]
Repeat forever: there is no “next” fraction after any fraction.

7Where this goes

  • Next (1.B.4): a fraction can also compare two amounts: 2/3 as “2 scoops for every 3 cups.” That’s a ratio, and equivalent fractions become equivalent ratios.
  • Later in this book: 1.B.9 shows that some points on the line (like √2) are not fractions at all, which is how the real numbers are born. Repeating decimals like 0.999… lead to limits (Chapter 6), and every probability is a fraction between 0 and 1 (Chapter 9).
  • In the world: recipes (¾ cup), rulers marked in sixteenths of an inch, money (a cent is 1/100 of a dollar), music (a quarter note), and percentages (a fraction out of 100).

8Words to know

FractionA number a/b: cut each whole into b equal pieces and take a of them. A point on the number line.
NumeratorThe top number: how many pieces you take.
DenominatorThe bottom number: how many pieces each whole is cut into.
Unit fractionOne piece: 1/b.
Improper fraction / mixed numberA fraction bigger than 1, like 7/3, and the same amount written as wholes plus a fraction, 2⅓.
Equivalent fractionsDifferent names for the same point: ½ = 2/4 = 3/6.
Common denominatorA shared piece size, needed to add fractions.
DecimalA number written by zooming in by tens: tenths, hundredths, thousandths…
Terminating / repeating decimalA decimal that stops (0.75) or one whose digits repeat forever (0.3).
Rational numberAny number that can be written as a fraction of whole numbers. Its decimal always terminates or repeats.

9Check yourself

Four kinds of questions: place a fraction on the line, convert between fractions and decimals, decide whether two fractions are equivalent, and find a fraction in between. Fractions like 3/8 are fine as answers.
