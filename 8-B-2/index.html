Chapter 8 · Calculus

Derivatives

The derivative is the slope of a curve at a single point: how fast the output is changing right now. Zoom in on a smooth curve and it looks straight; the derivative is the slope of that straight line.

1Slope at a point

A straight line has one slope everywhere. A curve’s steepness changes as you move along it. To measure it at one point (a, f(a)), take a second point a little to the right, at a + h, and draw the secant line through both. Its slope is rise over run:

\[ \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \]
The difference quotient: the average rate of change over [a, a + h].

Now let h shrink toward 0. The second point slides toward the first, the secant swings into the tangent line, and the difference quotient heads to a limit (8.B.1). That limit is the derivative of f at a, written f′(a) (“f prime of a”) or df/dx. For f(x) = x²: ((a + h)² − a²)/h = (2ah + h²)/h = 2a + h, which heads to 2a. So the slope of x² at any point a is 2a.

Doing this at every point gives a new function, f′, whose height at each x is the slope of f there. Where f climbs, f′ is positive; where f falls, f′ is negative; at the top of a hill or bottom of a valley, f′ = 0.

2The basic rules

Working out a limit every time would be slow, so we work out a few once and build everything else from rules.

FunctionDerivativeWhy, in a sentence
c (a constant)0A flat line has slope 0.
xnn·xn−1The power rule: x² → 2x, x³ → 3x², √x = x½ → ½x−½.
c·f(x)c·f′(x)Stretching a graph up by c multiplies every slope by c.
f(x) + g(x)f′(x) + g′(x)The sum rule: rates of change add.
sin x, cos xcos x, −sin xSlopes of a wave are another wave, a quarter-turn ahead (uses lim sin h / h = 1).
exexThe one function that is its own slope: that’s what makes e special.

So the derivative of 3x⁴ − 2x + 5 is 12x³ − 2: each term separately, constants vanish.

3Product, quotient and chain

Product rule. Think of u·v as the area of a rectangle with sides u and v. Nudge both sides: the area grows by a strip u·Δv along one side, a strip v·Δu along the other, and a tiny corner Δu·Δv that vanishes in the limit. So (uv)′ = u′v + uv′, not u′v′. The quotient rule follows from it: (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v².

Chain rule. For a function inside a function, like sin(x²), rates multiply. Picture gears: if the middle gear turns 3 times for each turn of the first, and the last turns 2 times for each turn of the middle, the last turns 6 times for each turn of the first. So (f(g(x)))′ = f′(g(x))·g′(x): the outside’s rate (measured at the inside) times the inside’s rate.

    4Reading the shape: f′ and f″

    The sign of f′ tells you the direction: f′ > 0 means f is increasing, f′ < 0 means decreasing. Where f′ = 0 (or doesn’t exist) is a critical point: often a peak (local maximum) or a valley (local minimum), where the tangent is flat.

    The derivative of the derivative is the second derivative f″. It measures how the slope itself changes: f″ > 0 means the slope is increasing, so the graph bends upward like a cup (concave up); f″ < 0 means it bends downward like a cap (concave down). Where the bending switches is an inflection point.

    In motion, these have names. If s(t) is position, then s′(t) is velocity (how fast position changes) and s″(t) is acceleration (how fast velocity changes). For a spreading rumour, the number who’ve heard it grows fastest at the inflection point, halfway to everyone.

    The chain rule also answers “how fast?” questions where two quantities are tied together. Pour water into a cone-shaped cup at a steady rate. The volume rises steadily, but the water level doesn’t: near the narrow tip a little water raises the level a lot, and higher up the same amount spreads over a wider surface. With V = πh³/12 for this cup, the chain rule gives dV/dt = (πh²/4)·dh/dt, so the level rises at dh/dt = 4·(pour rate)/(πh²).

    5Traps and non-examples

    Compare

    Easy to get wrong

    ✕ A corner has no derivative

    |x| at 0: from the left every secant has slope −1, from the right +1. The limit doesn’t exist, so there’s no tangent. Continuous doesn’t imply differentiable.

    ✕ (uv)′ ≠ u′v′

    x·x = x² has derivative 2x, but u′v′ = 1·1 = 1. The rectangle grows along both sides: u′v + uv′ = x + x.

    ✕ f′ = 0 isn’t always a peak or valley

    x³ has a flat tangent at 0, but keeps climbing on both sides: it’s an inflection point. Check the sign of f′ on each side.

    6Now, in symbols

    \[ f'(a) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}, \qquad \text{tangent line: } y = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) \]
    \[ \frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1},\quad \frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x,\quad \frac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x,\quad \frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x,\quad \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac1x \]
    \[ (uv)' = u'v + uv', \qquad \Big(\frac uv\Big)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x) = \frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx} \]
    \[ f' > 0 \Rightarrow f \text{ increasing}, \quad f'' > 0 \Rightarrow f \text{ concave up}, \qquad s'(t) = v(t),\ s''(t) = v'(t) = a(t) \]

    A computer algebra system applies the rules for you, and a quick numerical check catches mistakes:

    import sympy as sp                                   # symbolic maths

x = sp.symbols('x')                                  # the variable
f = x**2 * sp.sin(x)                                 # a product
print(sp.diff(f, x))                                 # x**2*cos(x) + 2*x*sin(x): the product rule
print(sp.diff(sp.sin(x**2), x))                      # 2*x*cos(x**2): the chain rule
g = sp.lambdify(x, f)                                # turn f into an ordinary Python function
h = 1e-6                                             # a tiny step
print((g(1 + h) - g(1)) / h)                         # ≈ 2.2232: the difference quotient at x = 1
print(float(sp.diff(f, x).subs(x, 1)))               # 2.2232...: the exact derivative agrees

    8Words to know

    Derivativef′(a): the instantaneous rate of change of f at a; the slope of the tangent line there.
    Difference quotient(f(a + h) − f(a))/h: the average rate of change, the slope of a secant.
    Secant lineA line through two points of a curve.
    Tangent lineThe line through a point with the curve’s slope there: the limit of secants.
    Instantaneous rate of changeThe rate at one moment, found as a limit of average rates.
    DifferentiableHaving a derivative at a point. Corners, jumps and vertical tangents are not.
    Leibniz notationdy/dx: another way to write the derivative, as a ratio of tiny changes.
    Power rule(xn)′ = nxn−1.
    Sum / constant multiple ruleDifferentiate term by term; constants come along for the ride.
    Product rule(uv)′ = u′v + uv′.
    Quotient rule(u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v².
    Chain rule(f(g(x)))′ = f′(g(x))·g′(x): rates multiply.
    Critical pointWhere f′ = 0 or doesn’t exist: candidates for peaks and valleys.
    Local maximum / minimumA peak or valley: higher (lower) than all nearby points.
    Second derivativef″: the derivative of f′; how the slope is changing.
    Concave up / downBending like a cup (f″ > 0) or a cap (f″ < 0).
    Inflection pointWhere the concavity switches.
    Velocity / accelerationThe first and second derivatives of position.
