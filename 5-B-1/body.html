Chapter 5 · Trigonometry

Angles and Radians

An angle is an amount of turning. Degrees cut a full turn into 360 slices; radians measure the turn with the circle’s own radius, wrapped around its edge like a string.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • See an angle as a turn from an initial side to a terminal side, including turns past a full circle and clockwise (negative) turns.
  • Measure the same turn in degrees and in radians, and convert between them.
  • Understand why a full turn is 2π radians: it takes 2π radius-lengths of string to go around a circle.
  • Use arc length = radius × angle (in radians), and spot coterminal angles that land in the same place.

1An angle is a turn

Stand facing a wall, then turn to face a different direction. How much you turned is an angle. In a picture, an angle starts with a ray called the initial side, pointing right, and rotates around a point called the vertex to a new position, the terminal side.

By tradition, turning counterclockwise (the opposite way to a clock’s hands) counts as positive, and turning clockwise counts as negative. And nothing stops you from turning more than once around: a skateboarder’s “540” is one and a half full spins.

2Two rulers for turning

Degrees cut one full turn into 360 equal slices. Nobody knows for certain why 360, but it’s a wonderfully divisible number (it splits evenly into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, eighths, tenths and twelfths), and ancient astronomers liked that a year has roughly 360 days. A quarter turn is 90°, a half turn is 180°.

Radians come from the circle itself. Take a piece of string exactly as long as the circle’s radius (center to edge), and lay it along the edge. The angle it covers is 1 radian, about 57.3°. How many pieces of string fit around the whole circle? A little more than 6: exactly 2π ≈ 6.283. So a full turn is 2π radians, a half turn is π radians, and a quarter turn is π/2.

3Converting between degrees and radians

Both rulers measure the same full turn, so 360° = 2π radians, or, halving both, 180° = π radians. That single fact is all you need. Degrees and radians are in a proportional relationship (1.B.5): to go from degrees to radians, multiply by π/180; to go back, multiply by 180/π.

4Arc length: why radians are so useful

Here’s the payoff. The angle in radians counts radius-lengths of string along the edge. So if the radius is 3 cm and the angle is 2 radians, the curved edge (the arc) is 2 radius-lengths long: 2 × 3 = 6 cm. Arc length = radius × angle in radians. With degrees, there’s no such simple rule.

5Different angles, same place

Turn 270° counterclockwise, or 90° clockwise (−90°): you end up facing the same way. Angles whose terminal sides land in the same place are called coterminal. They always differ by a whole number of full turns: 360°, 720°, … (or 2π, 4π, … in radians). A common mistake is to think −90° and 90° are the same place. They’re mirror images: one points down, the other up.

6Now, in symbols

In the explorer, the angle was θ (the Greek letter theta) and the radius was r. The two rulers are tied together by one full turn:

\[ 360^\circ = 2\pi \text{ radians} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \theta_{\text{rad}} = \theta_{\text{deg}} \times \frac{\pi}{180}, \quad \theta_{\text{deg}} = \theta_{\text{rad}} \times \frac{180}{\pi} \]
Example: \(135^\circ \times \tfrac{\pi}{180} = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\) radians.

The radius string gives the arc length \(s\):

\[ s = r\,\theta \qquad (\theta \text{ in radians}) \]
For a fixed angle, \(s\) is proportional to \(r\). For a fixed circle, \(s\) is proportional to \(\theta\). Either way, the constant of proportionality is the other quantity.

And coterminal angles differ by whole turns:

\[ \theta \quad\text{and}\quad \theta + 360^\circ \cdot n \qquad (n = \ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots) \]
In radians: \(\theta + 2\pi n\).

7Where this goes

  • Next (5.B.2): put a point where the terminal side meets a circle of radius 1. Its across and up coordinates are the cosine and sine of the angle: the start of trigonometry.
  • Later in this book: waves and repeating patterns (5.B.4) are measured in radians. In Calculus, the slope of the sine curve is exactly cosine only in radians, which is why mathematicians almost always use them. In Linear Algebra, a rotation of the plane by θ is a matrix built from cos θ and sin θ.
  • In the world: clock hands, steering wheels, the spin of a bike wheel (how far the bike rolls is radius × angle turned), skateboard and figure-skating spins (360, 540, 720), and the angle of a ramp.

8Words to know

AngleAn amount of turning, from an initial side to a terminal side around a vertex.
VertexThe point the angle turns around.
Initial side / terminal sideThe ray where the turn starts (pointing right) and the ray where it ends.
Degree (°)One 360th of a full turn.
RadianThe angle that wraps one radius-length of string along a circle’s edge. About 57.3°.
π (pi)About 3.14159: the number of radius-lengths in half a circle. A full turn is 2π radians.
Arc lengthThe length along the curved edge: s = r × θ, with θ in radians.
Coterminal anglesAngles that land in the same place, differing by whole turns: −90° and 270°.
Counterclockwise / clockwisePositive turning (against a clock’s hands) / negative turning (with them).

9Check yourself

Four kinds of questions: convert degrees ↔ radians, read an angle from a picture, find an arc length, and find a coterminal angle. Radians can be typed as decimals or with pi (like 3pi/4).
