Chapter 8 · Calculus

Power Series

A polynomial that never ends. For some values of x the sum makes sense, for others it doesn’t, and where it does you can treat it like any other function: plug in, differentiate, integrate, even solve equations with it.

Basics Undergraduate you are here Masters

On this page

  • Read a power series Σ ck(x − a)k as a function of x, and see that it converges on an interval around its centre.
  • Find the radius of convergence with the ratio or root test, then check the two endpoints separately to get the interval of convergence.
  • Differentiate and integrate term by term, and build new series by substitution and multiplication.
  • Use series to compute π, to count with generating functions, and to solve differential equations, including the Bessel function behind a drum’s sound.

1A polynomial that goes on forever

A power series centred at a is

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} c_k (x - a)^k = c_0 + c_1(x - a) + c_2(x - a)^2 + \cdots \]

The numbers ck are its coefficients. Pick a value of x and you get an ordinary series of numbers (8.U.1), which converges or doesn’t. So a power series defines a function, but only on the set of x where it converges. The simplest example is the geometric series: 1 + x + x² + … = 1/(1 − x) for −1 < x < 1, and nonsense elsewhere.

The set where a power series converges is always an interval centred at a. The reason: if it converges at some point at distance d from a, its terms there stay bounded, and at any point closer than d the terms are smaller by a geometric factor, so they converge too (absolutely). The half-width of that interval is the radius of convergence R: the series converges absolutely for |x − a| < R, diverges for |x − a| > R, and the two endpoints a ± R have to be checked one at a time. R can be 0 (only the centre works) or ∞ (every x works).

2Finding the radius, then the endpoints

Apply the ratio test from 8.U.1 to the terms ck(x − a)k: the ratio of neighbours is |ck+1/ck|·|x − a|. If |ck+1/ck| → L, the series converges when L|x − a| < 1, so

\[ R = \lim_{k\to\infty}\left|\frac{c_k}{c_{k+1}}\right| \quad\text{(ratio)}, \qquad \frac1R = \limsup_{k\to\infty}|c_k|^{1/k} \quad\text{(root, which always works)}. \]

Examples: for Σ xk/k!, |ck/ck+1| = k + 1 → ∞, so R = ∞. For Σ k!·xk it’s 1/(k + 1) → 0, so R = 0. For Σ (x − 1)k/(k·3k) it’s 3(k + 1)/k → 3. The table in the explorer shows both ratios homing in on R.

The tests say nothing at |x − a| = R, so plug each endpoint in and use the series tests directly. Σ xk/k at x = 1 is the harmonic series (diverges) and at x = −1 the alternating harmonic series (converges): interval [−1, 1). Σ xk/k² converges at both ends: [−1, 1]. Σ xk converges at neither: (−1, 1).

3Calculus one term at a time

Inside its radius a power series can be treated like a polynomial: differentiate term by term or integrate term by term, and the new series has the same radius and equals the derivative or integral of the function. (Endpoints can change: differentiating can lose one, integrating can gain one.) Combined with substitution (replace x by −x, x², 2x, …) and multiplying by powers of x, the geometric series alone gives a whole family:

\[ \frac{1}{(1-x)^2} = \sum (k+1)x^k, \qquad \ln(1+x) = \sum_{k\ge1} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}x^k}{k}, \qquad \arctan x = \sum \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2k+1}. \]

This is also how to get series for functions with no elementary antiderivative. The bell-curve area ∫₀x e−t² dt has no formula in elementary functions, but substituting −t² into the series for et and integrating term by term gives x − x³/3 + x⁵/10 − …, which converges everywhere. Two series can also be multiplied like polynomials (the Cauchy product): (1 + x + x² + …)² collects 1, 2, 3, … ways of making each power, giving Σ(k + 1)xk again.

4Series as unknowns: solving differential equations

Suppose you didn’t know ex and wanted a function with y′ = y and y(0) = 1. Guess y = Σ ckxk. Then y′ = Σ (k + 1)ck+1xk, and matching the coefficient of each power gives the recurrence relation ck+1 = ck/(k + 1). Starting from c0 = 1 it gives ck = 1/k!: the exponential series, found from the equation alone. The same trick on y″ = −y produces cos and sin.

Some important functions are defined this way, because their equation has no solution in familiar functions. When a round drumhead vibrates, the shape across its radius follows Bessel’s equation xy″ + y′ + xy = 0. The series solution is the Bessel function J₀(x) = 1 − x²/4 + x⁴/64 − …. Its zeros 2.405, 5.520, 8.654, … set the drum’s pitches, and because they are not evenly spaced (unlike a guitar string’s 1 : 2 : 3), a drum sounds less like a definite note.

5Computing π

Put x = 1 in the arctan series (an endpoint, where it still converges, just barely) and you get Leibniz’s formula π/4 = 1 − ⅓ + ⅕ − …. It is beautiful and hopeless: the error after n terms is about 1/(2n), so a million terms give only six correct digits. The fix is to evaluate arctan closer to the centre, where the powers shrink fast. Abraham Sharp used π = 6 arctan(1/√3), and John Machin in 1706 found Machin’s formula π/4 = 4 arctan(1/5) − arctan(1/239), which gains about 1.4 digits per term. Formulas like his computed π by hand to 100 digits, and versions of them broke records into the computer age.

6Generating functions: counting with series

Turn the idea around: instead of studying a function through its coefficients, study a sequence of numbers by packing it into a function. The generating function of a sequence a₀, a₁, a₂, … is Σ akxk. Take the Fibonacci numbers 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, …, where each is the sum of the two before. If F(x) is their generating function, the rule says F − xF − x²F = 1, so

\[ F(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x - x^2} = 1 + x + 2x^2 + 3x^3 + 5x^4 + 8x^5 + \cdots \]

The radius of convergence now carries information. The denominator vanishes at x = (√5 − 1)/2 ≈ 0.618, so R ≈ 0.618, and the coefficients must grow like (1/R)k = φk, with φ ≈ 1.618 the golden ratio. That is why the ratio of neighbouring Fibonacci numbers tends to φ.

7Traps and non-examples

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ “Same radius, so same interval”

ln(1 + x) = x − x²/2 + … converges at x = 1 (blue, settles on ln 2). Its derivative series 1 − x + x² − … has the same R = 1 but diverges at x = 1 (red, 1, 0, 1, 0, …).

✕ Checking only one endpoint

Σ xk/k: at x = −1 the partial sums settle (green), at x = 1 they climb forever (red). The radius is symmetric; the endpoint behaviour need not be.

✕ Ratio of coefficients when powers skip

Σ x2k/4k = Σ (x²/4)k: geometric in x², so it needs x² < 4. R = 2, not 4. Apply the ratio test to whole terms, not to the listed numbers.

8Now, in symbols

\[ f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} c_k (x-a)^k, \qquad \frac1R = \limsup_{k\to\infty} |c_k|^{1/k}, \qquad \text{converges absolutely for } |x-a|
\[ f'(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k\,c_k (x-a)^{k-1}, \qquad \int_a^x f = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{c_k}{k+1}(x-a)^{k+1}, \qquad \text{same } R \]
\[ \Big(\sum a_k x^k\Big)\Big(\sum b_k x^k\Big) = \sum_k \Big(\sum_{j=0}^{k} a_j b_{k-j}\Big) x^k \quad\text{(Cauchy product)}, \qquad c_k = \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!} \text{ (8.U.3)} \]
\[ \frac{\pi}{4} = 4\arctan\frac15 - \arctan\frac1{239}, \qquad J_0(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{(k!)^2}\Big(\frac{x}{2}\Big)^{2k}, \qquad \sum_{k} F_{k+1} x^k = \frac{1}{1-x-x^2} \]

A computer algebra system handles all of this, including reading counts off a generating function:

import sympy as sp                                         # symbolic maths

x, k = sp.symbols('x k')                                   # the variable and an index
print(sp.series(sp.atan(x), x, 0, 9))                      # x - x**3/3 + x**5/5 - x**7/7 + O(x**9)
print(sp.series(1 / (1 - x - x**2), x, 0, 10))             # 1 + x + 2x² + 3x³ + 5x⁴ + …: Fibonacci numbers
c = 1 / sp.factorial(k)                                    # coefficients of the exponential series
print(sp.limit(sp.Abs(c / c.subs(k, k + 1)), k, sp.oo))     # ratio |c_k / c_(k+1)| → oo, so R = ∞

y = sp.Function('y')                                       # an unknown function
print(sp.dsolve(y(x).diff(x, 2) + y(x), hint='2nd_power_series_ordinary', n=8))  # power-series solution of y'' = -y

10Words to know

Power seriesA series Σ ck(x − a)k: a function of x wherever it converges.
CoefficientThe number ck multiplying (x − a)k.
CentreThe point a the series is built around; it always converges there.
Radius of convergenceR: converges for |x − a| < R, diverges for |x − a| > R. Can be 0 or ∞.
Interval of convergenceAll x where it converges: (a − R, a + R) plus whichever endpoints work.
Endpointa ± R; must be tested separately.
Ratio formula for RR = lim |ck/ck+1|, when the limit exists.
Root formula for R1/R = lim sup |ck|1/k (Cauchy–Hadamard); always works.
Term-by-term differentiationDifferentiate each power; same radius.
Term-by-term integrationIntegrate each power; same radius.
SubstitutionReplacing x by an expression such as −x² in a known series.
Cauchy productMultiplying two power series by collecting equal powers.
Analytic functionA function equal to its power series near each point.
Recurrence relationA rule giving each coefficient from earlier ones, such as ck+1 = ck/(k + 1).
Bessel functionJ₀, the series solution of xy″ + y′ + xy = 0; describes drumheads and waves in cylinders.
Leibniz seriesπ/4 = 1 − ⅓ + ⅕ − …: arctan at its endpoint; very slow.
Machin’s formulaπ/4 = 4 arctan(1/5) − arctan(1/239): fast because both arguments are near 0.
Generating functionΣ akxk: a function that packs a sequence into its coefficients.
