Chapter 9 · Probability

Chance and Probability

A probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is. You can work it out by counting outcomes, or measure it by repeating an experiment many times, and the two answers meet.

1How likely is it?

Some things can’t happen: rolling a 7 on an ordinary die. Some things must happen: rolling a number from 1 to 6. Most things sit in between. Probability puts a number on “how likely”: 0 means impossible, 1 means certain, and ½ means an even chance, as likely to happen as not. Below ½ is unlikely, above ½ is likely.

The same probability can be written three ways, just as in 1.B.3: as a fraction (¼), a decimal (0.25) or a percent (25%). A weather forecast of “70% chance of rain” is the probability 0.7.

2Counting equally likely outcomes

Anything with an uncertain result, like rolling a die, is an experiment, and one go at it is a trial. Each possible result is an outcome, and the list of all outcomes is the sample space. For one die the sample space is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. An event is a group of outcomes we care about, such as “an even number” = {2, 4, 6}; those are its favourable outcomes.

When every outcome is equally likely (a fair die, a well-shuffled deck, a bag you can’t see into), the theoretical probability is a count:

\[ P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}, \qquad P(\text{even}) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac12. \]

The outcomes that are not in the event make up its complement. Every outcome is either in the event or not, so the two probabilities add to 1: P(not even) = 1 − ½ = ½. If the chance of rain is 70%, the chance of no rain is 30%.

3Counting versus doing it

Counting tells you a fair coin lands heads with probability ½. But flip it 10 times and you might get 7 heads. Flipping and recording is finding the experimental probability, also called the relative frequency:

\[ \text{relative frequency} = \frac{\text{number of times the event happened}}{\text{number of trials}}. \]

With a few trials the relative frequency jumps around. With many trials it settles down close to the theoretical probability. That pattern has a name, the law of large numbers, and it’s why casinos always win in the long run even though any one bet is a gamble. The result of each trial is random: you can’t predict it, but you can predict the long-run pattern.

Experiments are also how we find probabilities we can’t count: the chance a basketball player makes a free throw, or that a drawing pin lands point-up. A computer can run a million trials in a blink:

import random                                             # Python's random-number tools
rolls = [random.randint(1, 6) for _ in range(1_000_000)]   # roll a fair die a million times
sixes = rolls.count(6)                                    # how many of those rolls came up 6
print(sixes / len(rolls))                                 # relative frequency: very close to 1/6 ≈ 0.1667

4When outcomes aren’t equally likely

“Favourable ÷ total” only works when every outcome has the same chance. A spinner with a big red half and three small sectors has four outcomes, but red isn’t ¼: it’s ½, because probability follows the size of the sector. A biased (loaded) die is weighted to land on 6 more often. A free throw has two outcomes, make or miss, but a good player makes 70% or more. Two outcomes does not mean 50–50. For these, the probability comes from the shape of the object or from data, and an experiment is how you check.

5What a forecast means

“70% chance of rain” doesn’t mean it will rain for 70% of the day, or that the forecaster is 70% sure. It means: of all the days with a forecast like this one, it rains on about 7 out of 10. A single dry day doesn’t prove the forecast wrong, and neither does a single wet one. Try the “70% rain forecast” preset above: each trial is one such day.

6Traps

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ “Tails is due”

After five heads in a row, the next flip is still 50–50. A coin has no memory. (This mistake is called the gambler’s fallacy.)

✕ “Two outcomes, so 50–50”

A raffle with 50 tickets: you either win or you don’t. That’s two outcomes, but your chance is 1/50 = 2%, not 50%. The outcomes aren’t equally likely.

✕ “10 flips gives 5 heads”

Probability ½ means about half in the long run. In 10 flips, exactly 5 heads happens only about 25% of the time; 4 or 6 are almost as common.

7Now, in symbols

Write S for the sample space, A for an event, and |A| for the number of outcomes in it.

\[ 0 \le P(A) \le 1, \qquad P(\text{impossible}) = 0, \qquad P(S) = 1 \]
\[ \text{equally likely outcomes: } P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|S|}, \qquad P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A) \]
\[ \text{after } n \text{ trials with } A \text{ happening } k \text{ times: } \quad \frac{k}{n} \;\longrightarrow\; P(A) \ \text{ as } n \text{ grows.} \]
