Chapter 7 · Linear Algebra

Matrix Multiplication: One Move After Another

Do one move, then another, and the result is again a single move with its own matrix. Multiplying matrices is how you find it, and why the order you do things in matters.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • See that two moves in a row, A then B, make one move, written BA (read right to left).
  • Compute a matrix product column by column (B times each column of A) and with the row · column shortcut.
  • Learn that order matters: usually BA ≠ AB, and spot the moves that commute.
  • Repeat a move with powers (A², A³, …), meet the identity matrix as “do nothing,” and see when the sizes of two matrices let you multiply them.

1Two moves make one move

In 7.B.4 a matrix was a move of the plane, recorded by where î and ĵ land. Now do two moves in a row: first A, then B. Grid lines were still straight, parallel and evenly spaced after A, and B keeps them that way. So the combined move is linear too, and it has its own matrix.

To find it, follow î. Move A sends it to the first column of A. Then B moves that arrow, so î ends up at B × (column 1 of A), a matrix–vector product from 7.B.4. The same goes for ĵ and column 2. Those two end points are the columns of the combined matrix.

We write the combined move as BA, with the first move on the right, closest to the point it acts on: (BA)v = B(Av). It’s the same right-to-left reading as putting one function inside another, like f(g(x)), where g happens first. Combining moves this way is called composition, and the combined matrix is the matrix product.

2Computing the product

Column by column. Each column of BA is B times the matching column of A. That’s two matrix–vector products, exactly the ones from 7.B.4.

Row · column shortcut. Write out those matrix–vector products and a pattern appears: the entry in row r, column c of BA is the dot product (7.B.3) of row r of B with column c of A. Slide along the row while sliding down the column, multiply matching entries, add.

Example: B = 1201, A = 3012
Column 1 of BAB·[3, 1] = [1·3 + 2·1, 0·3 + 1·1] = [5, 1]
Column 2 of BAB·[0, 2] = [1·0 + 2·2, 0·0 + 1·2] = [4, 2]
Row · column checktop-left = (row 1 of B)·(column 1 of A) = 1·3 + 2·1 = 5
SoBA = [[5, 4], [1, 2]] (rows listed top to bottom)

One trap: multiplying matrices is not multiplying matching entries. That would give [[3, 0], [0, 2]] here, which is not where the moves send anything.

3Order matters

Put on socks, then shoes, and you’re ready to go. Shoes, then socks, and you’re not. Moves of the plane are the same. Turning and then stretching sideways is not the same as stretching sideways and then turning: try the first two presets above, or press Swap order. In general BA ≠ AB: matrix multiplication is not commutative.

Some pairs of moves do commute (give the same result in either order): two turns, two stretches along the axes, a half turn with anything, and any move paired with the identity matrix I (the “do nothing” move, with columns [1, 0] and [0, 1]). 4Repeating a move: powers

Doing the same move n times in a row is the power An = A · A ⋯ A. Here order can’t matter, since every step is the same move. Turning by 30° twelve times is a full turn: A¹² = I. A mirror done twice puts everything back, so a mirror undoes itself: M² = I. Moves that undo other moves are the inverse matrices of 7.B.7. And A⁰ = I: doing a move zero times is doing nothing.

5Chains of tables, and which sizes fit

Matrix products aren’t only about pictures. A toy company sells two building kits, a Rover and a Crane. Each kit needs parts (wheels, arms, panels), and each part is snapped together from red and blue bricks. Two tables describe this. P says how many of each part go into each kit; Q says how many bricks of each color go into each part.

P has 3 rows (parts) and 2 columns (kits): it’s a 3×2 matrix, read “3 by 2,” rows first. Q has 2 rows (colors) and 3 columns (parts): 2×3. The product QP answers “how many bricks of each color per kit?” Each entry is a row of Q dotted with a column of P, and that only works when they have the same length. So a product needs the inner sizes to match: (2×3)(3×2) is fine, and the answer has the outer sizes, 2×2. (7.B.11 does sizes in full.)

6The same move, year after year

Two towns, Riverton and Hillside, swap people every year: some fraction of Riverton moves to Hillside, and some fraction of Hillside moves to Riverton. One year is a matrix M whose first column says where Riverton’s people end up (stay, or move) and whose second column says the same for Hillside. Ten years is M¹⁰. The populations settle down to a balance where the flows in each direction cancel. Finding that balance directly is a job for eigenvectors (Undergraduate tier).

8Now, in symbols

\[ (BA)\,\mathbf{v} = B\,(A\,\mathbf{v}), \qquad BA = \big[\, B\mathbf{a}_1 \;\; B\mathbf{a}_2 \,\big] \]
Do A, then B. Each column of BA is B times a column of A.
\[ \begin{bmatrix} p & q \\ r & s \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} pa + qc & pb + qd \\ ra + sc & rb + sd \end{bmatrix} \]
Row · column: each entry is a row of the left matrix dotted with a column of the right one.
\[ AB \ne BA \text{ in general}, \qquad AI = IA = A, \qquad (AB)C = A(BC), \qquad A^n = \underbrace{A\,A\cdots A}_{n} \]
Order matters; the identity does nothing; grouping doesn’t matter (you can combine any neighbors first); powers repeat a move.
\[ (m\times \mathbf{n})\,(\mathbf{n}\times p) = (m\times p) \]
The inner sizes must match; the answer has the outer sizes.

For the towns, a computer finds a power like M⁵⁰ in a blink (it squares M repeatedly instead of multiplying 50 times):

import numpy as np                                   # NumPy: arrays of numbers
M = np.array([[0.9, 0.2],                            # top row: Riverton keeps 90%, gains 20% of Hillside
              [0.1, 0.8]])                           # bottom row: Hillside gains 10% of Riverton, keeps 80%
start = np.array([100, 20])                          # thousands of people in Riverton and Hillside now
for n in [1, 10, 50]:                                # look 1, 10 and 50 years ahead
    Mn = np.linalg.matrix_power(M, n)                # M multiplied by itself n times
    print(n, (Mn @ start).round(1))                  # populations after n years -> settles near [80, 40]

9Where this goes

  • Next (7.B.6): the determinant. If one move multiplies areas by 2 and the next by 3, the pair multiplies them by 6: determinants multiply when matrices do.
  • Later in this book: undoing a move (inverse matrices, 7.B.7, where A−1A = I), solving systems of equations (7.B.8–7.B.9), matrices of every size (7.B.11), and the long-run behavior of repeated moves (eigenvectors, Undergraduate tier; Markov chains in probability).
  • In the world: every 3D game and animated film chains camera, turn and perspective matrices; robot arms chain one matrix per joint; and a neural network is a chain of matrix multiplications with a simple bend between them.

10Words to know

CompositionDoing one move after another. A then B is written BA.
Matrix productBA: the single matrix for “A, then B.” Its columns are B times the columns of A.
Row · column ruleEntry (row r, column c) of BA = (row r of B) · (column c of A).
Order mattersIn general AB ≠ BA: doing the moves in the other order gives a different move.
CommuteTwo matrices commute when AB = BA, like two turns or two axis stretches.
Identity matrixI, with columns [1, 0] and [0, 1]: the “do nothing” move. AI = IA = A.
PowerAn: the move A done n times in a row. A⁰ = I.
Size (m×n)A matrix with m rows and n columns. A product needs matching inner sizes: (m×n)(n×p) = (m×p).
