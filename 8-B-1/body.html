Chapter 8 · Calculus

Limits

A limit asks where a function is heading, not where it is. It’s the idea that lets calculus talk about an instant of time, a single point on a curve, or what happens in the long run.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Read limx→a f(x) = L as “f(x) gets as close as you like to L when x is close enough to a”, and see the precise ε–δ version.
  • Use one-sided limits, and recognise when a limit does not exist: jumps, blow-ups to infinity, and endless wiggling.
  • Compute limits by plugging in, and by factoring and cancelling when you get 0/0.
  • Find limits at infinity (horizontal asymptotes) and define continuity: the limit equals the value.

1Where is it heading?

Take f(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1). At x = 1 it is 0/0, which means nothing: the function has a hole there. But try numbers near 1: f(0.9) = 1.9, f(0.99) = 1.99, f(1.01) = 2.01, f(1.001) = 2.001. As x closes in on 1 from either side, f(x) closes in on 2. We say the limit of f(x) as x approaches 1 is 2, and write limx→1 f(x) = 2.

The key point: the limit doesn’t care what happens at a, only near it. The function might be undefined at a (a hole), or defined but with a value that doesn’t fit (a point moved off the curve), and the limit is the same. If you zoom in far enough around x = a, the graph squeezes toward a single height L: that height is the limit.

How close is “close”? The precise version is a challenge game. Someone names a tolerance ε (epsilon) around L, as tiny as they like. You must answer with a window δ (delta) around a so that every x in the window (except possibly a itself) gives f(x) within ε of L. If you can always win, however small ε is, the limit is L.

2One-sided limits, and when limits fail

Sometimes the left and the right disagree. The left-hand limit limx→a⁻ f(x) only uses x < a; the right-hand limit limx→a⁺ only uses x > a. The two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal. Try the presets in the explorer above; there are three classic ways for a limit to not exist:

  • A jump. A parking fee that jumps from $2 to $3 at the one-hour mark: from the left it heads to 2, from the right to 3. No single L works.
  • A blow-up. 1/(x − 1)² grows without bound near 1. We write lim = ∞ to say how it fails, but ∞ isn’t a number, so the limit still doesn’t exist. The line x = 1 is a vertical asymptote.
  • Endless wiggling. sin(1/x) swings between −1 and 1 faster and faster as x → 0 and never settles. Zoom in all you like: it never squeezes to one height.

3Computing limits

For most functions you meet (polynomials, and fractions of them where the bottom isn’t 0) the graph has no gaps, so the limit is just the value: plug in. The limit laws say limits behave well with arithmetic: the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, likewise for differences, products, constant multiples, and quotients (as long as the bottom’s limit isn’t 0).

The interesting case is when plugging in gives 0/0. That isn’t an answer; it’s a signal that something cancels. Factor the top and bottom, cancel the common factor (allowed, because x ≠ a while we approach), then plug in. The cancelled factor is exactly what made the hole.

    4Limits at infinity, and continuity

    We can also ask where a function heads as x grows without bound: limx→∞ f(x). Hot coffee cools toward room temperature; the graph flattens out along a horizontal asymptote. For a fraction of polynomials, only the leading terms (highest powers) matter when x is huge: (3x² + 1)/(x² + 5) behaves like 3x²/x² = 3. If the bottom’s power is bigger, the limit is 0; if the top’s is bigger, it grows without bound.

    Finally, continuity. A function is continuous at a when three things hold: f(a) exists, the limit at a exists, and they are equal. In pictures: you can draw through a without lifting your pen. Holes, moved points, jumps and blow-ups are all discontinuities. Polynomials are continuous everywhere, which is why plugging in works for them.

    5Why calculus needs limits

    A speedometer shows your speed right now. But speed is distance ÷ time, and in a single instant you travel 0 distance in 0 time: 0/0 again. The way out is a limit: measure the average speed over a short interval, then a shorter one, then shorter still, and see where the answers head. On the graph of distance against time, each average speed is the slope of a secant line through two points, and as the interval shrinks the secant turns into the tangent line. Zoom in far enough on a smooth curve and it looks straight: its slope there is the instantaneous speed. That limit is the derivative, the subject of 8.B.2.

    Limits also settle an old puzzle. To reach a wall, first walk half the way, then half of what’s left, then half again. That’s infinitely many steps. Do you ever arrive? The distances walked, ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + …, get as close as you like to the whole distance: their limit is exactly the full length. Infinitely many steps can add up to something finite, which is the idea behind infinite series (8.U.1).

    6Traps and non-examples

    Compare

    Easy to get wrong

    ✕ The limit is not “the value at a”

    g(x) = x² except g(2) = 1. Near 2 the graph heads to 4, so the limit is 4, though g(2) = 1. Plugging in only works where the function is continuous.

    ✕ 0/0 doesn’t mean “no limit”

    (x² − 9)/(x − 3) is 0/0 at 3, but it equals x + 3 everywhere else, so the limit is 6. 0/0 means “simplify first”, not “undefined forever”.

    ✓ Wiggling can still settle

    sin(1/x) has no limit at 0, but x·sin(1/x) does: the wiggles are squeezed between −|x| and |x|, which both head to 0. Its limit is 0.

    7Now, in symbols

    \[ \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \iff \text{for every } \varepsilon > 0 \text{ there is a } \delta > 0 \text{ such that } 0 < |x - a| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon \]
    The ε–δ definition: any output tolerance ε can be met by some input window δ. “0 < |x − a|” leaves out x = a itself.
    \[ \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = L = \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) \]
    \[ \lim (f \pm g) = \lim f \pm \lim g, \quad \lim (fg) = \lim f \cdot \lim g, \quad \lim \frac{f}{g} = \frac{\lim f}{\lim g} \ \ (\lim g \ne 0) \]
    The limit laws, when the limits on the right exist.
    \[ \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} = \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x - 3} = \lim_{x\to 3}(x + 3) = 6, \qquad \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1 \]
    \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{a_n x^n + \dots}{b_m x^m + \dots} = \begin{cases} a_n/b_m & n = m \\ 0 & n < m \\ \pm\infty & n > m \end{cases} \qquad f \text{ continuous at } a \iff \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a) \]

    A computer can do the tables for you, and a symbolic package can find limits exactly:

    import sympy as sp                                  # symbolic maths: exact answers, not decimals

x = sp.symbols('x')                                 # the variable
print(sp.limit((x**2 - 9) / (x - 3), x, 3))        # 6: the 0/0 at x = 3 cancels
print(sp.limit(sp.sin(x) / x, x, 0))                # 1: the famous one
print(sp.limit((3*x**2 + 1) / (x**2 + 5), x, sp.oo)) # 3: ratio of the leading terms
print(sp.limit(1 / (x - 1), x, 1, '+'), sp.limit(1 / (x - 1), x, 1, '-'))   # oo -oo: one-sided blow-ups

    8Where this goes

    • Next (8.B.2): the derivative, the limit of average rates of change: the slope of a curve at a single point, and the speed at a single instant.
    • Later in this book: integrals (8.B.3) are limits of sums of thinner and thinner slices; infinite series (8.U.1) are limits of partial sums like Zeno’s walk; and every theorem of real analysis rests on the ε–δ definition.
    • In the world: instantaneous speed and acceleration, long-run behaviour (cooling, saturation, steady states), and every measurement that gets “as precise as you need” by taking more care.

    9Words to know

    Limitlimx→a f(x) = L: f(x) gets as close as you like to L for all x close enough to a (but not equal).
    ApproachGet closer and closer to a value without needing to reach it; written →.
    HoleA single missing point in a graph, often from a factor that cancels.
    ε–δ definitionThe precise meaning of a limit: every tolerance ε on outputs can be met by some window δ on inputs.
    One-sided limitThe limit from the left (x → a⁻) or from the right (x → a⁺) only.
    Does not exist (DNE)No single L works: one-sided limits differ, the function blows up, or it never settles.
    Infinite limitlim = ∞: the function grows without bound. A way of not existing, with a description.
    Vertical asymptoteA line x = a where the function blows up.
    Indeterminate form 0/0What plugging in gives when top and bottom both head to 0; a sign to simplify.
    Limit lawsLimits of sums, products and quotients are the sums, products and quotients of the limits.
    Limit at infinitylimx→∞ f(x): where the function heads as x grows without bound.
    Horizontal asymptoteA line y = L that the graph settles toward far out.
    Continuousf is continuous at a when limx→a f(x) = f(a): no hole, jump or blow-up.
    DiscontinuityA point where a function isn’t continuous: a hole, a moved point, a jump or an asymptote.
    Secant / tangent lineA secant joins two points of a curve; the tangent is its limit as the points merge.
