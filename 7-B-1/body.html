Chapter 7 · Linear Algebra

Vectors: Arrows and Lists

A vector is a move: so far this way, so far that way. Draw it and it’s an arrow; write it down and it’s a list of numbers. Linear algebra is the art of using both at once.

On this page

  • See a vector as an arrow (a move) and as a list of components [a, b], and switch between them.
  • Add vectors tip to tail, scale them by a number, and subtract to get the arrow from one tip to another.

  • See why any list of numbers, like a color or a shopping list, is a vector too.

1A vector is a move

A treasure map says: from the old oak, walk 3 paces east and 2 paces north. That instruction is a vector. You can draw it as an arrow from where you start to where you end, or write it as a list of two numbers:

v = 32  or, on one line,  v = [3, 2].

The numbers are the vector’s components: the first, a, is how far to move along the x direction (east is positive, west negative); the second, b, is how far along y (north positive, south negative). Written stacked like that, it’s a column vector.

A vector is a move, not a place. “3 east, 2 north” works from any starting point, so the same arrow can be drawn anywhere. When it starts at the origin (0, 0), its tip lands on the point (3, 2), which is why the list and the point look alike.

2Adding vectors: one move, then another

Walk u, then walk v starting where u ended. The single arrow from your start to your finish is the sum u + v. This is tip to tail: put the tail of v on the tip of u.

In the list form it’s even easier: add first to first and second to second. [3, 1] + [1, 2] = [3 + 1, 1 + 2] = [4, 3]. The east steps add up, and the north steps add up.

The same idea runs the physical world. A boat aiming east while a current pushes it south actually travels along the sum of the two velocities. Two people pulling a sled with ropes in different directions move it along the sum of their forces.

3Scaling: stretch, squeeze, flip

Multiplying a vector by a plain number k stretches it, just as multiplying stretched the number line in 1.B.2. 2v is two copies of v laid end to end: same direction, twice as long. ½v is half as long. And −1 · v points the opposite way: walk the same move backwards. A plain number used this way is called a scalar, because it scales.

In the list, every component gets multiplied: 2 · [3, 1] = [6, 2]. Double the wind and both its east and north parts double.

4Subtracting: the arrow from one tip to another

You’re standing at the tip of a; the treasure is at the tip of b. What move gets you there? It’s b − a, the arrow from the tip of a to the tip of b. Check: walk a, then b − a, and you’re at b, because a + (b − a) = b.

Order matters: a − b is the same arrow pointing the other way. And in the list form you subtract entry by entry: [5, 4] − [2, 1] = [3, 3].

5How long is a vector?

The walk along x and the walk along y meet at a right angle, so the arrow is the long side (hypotenuse) of a right triangle. The Pythagorean theorem gives its length, called its magnitude, written ‖v‖:

‖v‖ = √(a² + b²)

For [3, 4] that’s √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5. Signs don’t matter, since squaring makes them positive: [−3, 4] is just as long. And the length is shorter than the walk: 5 beats 3 + 4 = 7, because the straight arrow cuts the corner.

6Vectors are lists, so lists are vectors

Nothing about the list form needs exactly two numbers, or arrows at all. A grocery order for two days is a list, and the total order is the sum, entry by entry:

appleseggsmilk (L)
Monday461
Tuesday2122
Total6183

That’s [4, 6, 1] + [2, 12, 2] = [6, 18, 3]: vector addition. Ordering the same thing every day for a week is 7 · [4, 6, 1]: scaling. A list of three numbers is a vector in 3 dimensions; a list of 100 numbers is a vector in 100 dimensions. We can’t draw a 100-dimensional arrow, but the arithmetic is exactly the same.

Every color on your screen is a vector too: [red, green, blue], each from 0 to 255. Adding two colors of light, or averaging two paints, is vector arithmetic.

7Now, in symbols

Vectors are usually written in bold (v) or with an arrow (\(\vec v\)). With u = [\(u_1, u_2\)] and v = [\(v_1, v_2\)] and a scalar \(k\):

\[ \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ u_2 + v_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad k\,\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} k\,v_1 \\ k\,v_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 - a_1 \\ b_2 - a_2 \end{bmatrix} \]
Everything happens entry by entry. Subtraction is adding \((-1)\,\mathbf{a}\).
\[ \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}, \qquad \lVert k\,\mathbf{v} \rVert = |k|\,\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \]
Scaling by \(k\) multiplies the length by \(|k|\): \(-2\mathbf{v}\) is twice as long as \(\mathbf{v}\), just pointing the other way.
\[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n, \qquad \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2} \]
A list of \(n\) real numbers is a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), “n-dimensional space.” The same rules hold in any dimension.

In code, a vector is simply an array, and libraries like NumPy do the entry-by-entry arithmetic for you:

import numpy as np                  # NumPy: Python's library for arrays of numbers
u = np.array([3, 1])                # the vector u = [3, 1]
v = np.array([1, 2])                # the vector v = [1, 2]
print(u + v)                        # tip to tail, entry by entry  -> [4 3]
print(2 * v)                        # scale every entry by 2       -> [2 4]
print(np.linalg.norm([3, 4]))       # length sqrt(3^2 + 4^2)       -> 5.0
day = np.array([4, 6, 1])           # apples, eggs, milk: a 3-D vector
print(7 * day)                      # a week of the same order     -> [28 42  7]

8Where this goes

  • Next (7.B.2): combine scaling and adding, like 2u + 3v, and ask which arrows you can reach. That’s a linear combination, and the set you can reach is the span.
  • Later in this book: the dot product (7.B.3) measures how much two vectors point the same way; matrices (7.B.4) move every vector at once; and in calculus (Chapter 8) the gradient is a vector pointing uphill.
  • In the world: velocities and forces in physics and games, positions in GPS and maps, colors on screens, and in data science every row of a spreadsheet, every image and every word in a language model is stored as a long list of numbers: a vector.

9Words to know

VectorA quantity with both a size and a direction: an arrow, or equivalently a list of numbers like [3, 2].
ComponentOne number in a vector’s list: how far it moves along one direction.
Column vectorA vector written as its components stacked in a column.
Tail / tipWhere an arrow starts and where it ends.
Tip to tailAdding vectors by starting the second where the first ends.
ScalarA plain number used to multiply (scale) a vector.
Magnitude (length)How long the arrow is: ‖v‖ = √(a² + b²).
Zero vector[0, 0]: the move that goes nowhere. Adding it changes nothing.
DimensionHow many components a vector has: 2 for arrows in the plane, 3 for space, any n for data.
