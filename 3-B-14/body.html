Logarithms

A logarithm answers one question: how many times do I multiply? It turns multiplying into adding, solves for exponents, and squeezes enormous ranges (from a whisper to a jet engine) onto a scale you can read.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Read logb y as “how many factors of b make y?”: the logarithm is the inverse of the exponential function.
  • See the curves y = bx and y = logb x as mirror images, and use the product, quotient and power rules.
  • Meet the special base e ≈ 2.718 and the natural logarithm ln, and switch bases with the change-of-base formula.
  • Solve exponential equations (doubling time, half-life) and read logarithmic scales: decibels, earthquake magnitudes and pH.

1How many times do I multiply?

Start at 1 and keep multiplying by 2: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. How many 2s does it take to reach 32? Five, because 2⁵ = 32. That count is the logarithm: log2 32 = 5, read “log base 2 of 32 is 5.” The number you multiply by is the base.

logb y = x   means exactly   bx = y

A logarithm is an exponent: the one you need to put on b to get y. The count doesn’t have to be a whole number. 2³ = 8 and 2⁴ = 16, so log2 10 is somewhere between 3 and 4 (about 3.32: three doublings and a bit more). It can be negative too: log2 (1/8) = −3, because going below 1 means dividing by 2 three times. And logb 1 = 0 for every base, since b⁰ = 1: zero multiplications.

2The log undoes the exponent

The function x ↦ bx takes an exponent and gives a value; logb takes the value back to the exponent. They are inverse functions (3.B.13): logb(bx) = x and blogb y = y. Swapping inputs and outputs flips a graph across the line y = x, which is why the two curves are mirror images.

Read the shapes from that mirror. bx is always positive, so the log only accepts positive inputs: logb 0 and logb(−5) don’t exist. The exponential climbs faster and faster, so the log climbs slower and slower: going from 10 to 100 to 1000 adds just 1 each time to log10. That slow growth is what makes logs good at taming huge numbers.

3The log rules: multiplying becomes adding

Exponents add when you multiply powers: 2³ · 2⁴ = 2⁷. Since logs are exponents, every exponent rule becomes a log rule:

RuleIn symbolsExampleBecause
Product rulelog(MN) = log M + log Nlog₂ 32 = log₂ 4 + log₂ 8 = 2 + 32² · 2³ = 2⁵
Quotient rulelog(M/N) = log M − log Nlog₁₀ 50 − log₁₀ 5 = log₁₀ 10 = 110ᵃ / 10ᶜ = 10ᵃ⁻ᶜ
Power rulelog(Mᵖ) = p · log Mlog₂ 8⁵ = 5 · log₂ 8 = 15(2³)⁵ = 2¹⁵

4The natural base e, and switching bases

Three bases matter most. Base 10 (the common logarithm, often written just log) counts digits: log10 1000 = 3. Base 2 counts doublings and halvings, which is how computers think. And base e = 2.71828…, written ln for the natural logarithm, is the one nature prefers.

Why such an odd number? Look at the dotted tangent line in the explorer: it shows how steeply bx climbs as it passes through (0, 1). For base 2 the slope is about 0.69, for base 3 about 1.10. For base e it is exactly 1, and in fact ex grows at a rate equal to its own value everywhere. Anything that grows in proportion to its own size (a bacteria colony, a chain reaction) is naturally written with e, and calculus (Chapter 8) is built around it. The slope of bx at 0 is ln b.

Calculators usually have only log (base 10) and ln. The change-of-base formula gets you any other base: logb y = ln y / ln b (or log y / log b, the same ratio). For example, log2 10 = ln 10 / ln 2 = 2.3026 / 0.6931 ≈ 3.32.

5Solving for the exponent

When the unknown is in the exponent, take a log of both sides and the power rule brings it down. To solve 2x = 50: x = log2 50 = ln 50 / ln 2 ≈ 5.64. Doubling and halving problems are exactly this kind of equation. A colony that doubles every d minutes has grown by a factor of 2t/d after t minutes, so reaching a factor F takes t = d · log2 F. A radioactive material with half-life h keeps (1/2)t/h of itself.

6Logarithmic scales

Some quantities span so many powers of ten that an ordinary scale is useless: the loudest sound you can stand is about a trillion times as intense as the quietest you can hear. A logarithmic scale records the logarithm instead, so each step means “multiply by 10” (or by some fixed factor). Small differences in the number mean big differences in the thing.

7Now, in symbols

\[ \log_b y = x \iff b^x = y \qquad (b > 0,\ b \ne 1,\ y > 0) \]
The definition: a logarithm is an exponent.
\[ \log_b(b^x) = x, \qquad b^{\log_b y} = y, \qquad \log_b 1 = 0, \qquad \log_b b = 1 \]
Inverses undo each other.
\[ \log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N, \quad \log_b\frac{M}{N} = \log_b M - \log_b N, \quad \log_b(M^p) = p\log_b M \]
The product, quotient and power rules.
\[ \log_b y = \frac{\ln y}{\ln b}, \qquad \ln y = \log_e y, \qquad e = \lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(1 + \frac1n\Big)^n \approx 2.71828 \]
Change of base, the natural log, and one definition of e (more in Chapter 8).
import math                                    # Python's built-in math tools
print(math.log(32, 2))                         # 5.0: log base 2 of 32 (2^5 = 32)
x = math.log(50) / math.log(2)                 # solve 2^x = 50 with change of base (ln 50 / ln 2)
print(round(x, 3), round(2 ** x, 6))           # 5.644  50.0  (check: 2^5.644 is 50)
half_life = 5730                               # carbon-14 half-life in years
age = half_life * math.log2(1 / 0.30)          # 30% left: solve (1/2)^(t/h) = 0.30 for t
print(round(age))                              # 9953 years old
print(10 ** ((110 - 60) / 10))                 # 100000.0: a 110 dB concert vs 60 dB talk, in intensity
