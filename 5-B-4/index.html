Waves and Periodic Functions

Walk a point around a circle and plot its height over time: out comes a wave. Stretch it, squeeze it, lift it and slide it, and the same shape describes tides, Ferris wheels, daylight and sound.

On this page

  • See the sine wave unroll from a point going around the unit circle, and cosine as the same wave, shifted.
  • Name the parts of a wave: amplitude, midline, period, frequency and phase shift.
  • Read and write a wave as y = A sin(B(x − C)) + D.

1Things that repeat

A lot of the world comes back around. The tide rises and falls about twice a day. A seat on a Ferris wheel climbs, peaks, drops and climbs again. The number of daylight hours swings from summer to winter and back every year. A guitar string vibrates back and forth hundreds of times a second.

A pattern that repeats exactly, over and over, is called a periodic function. The length of one full repeat is its period. And the most important repeating shape of all comes straight from the circle you met in 5.B.1.

2A circle, unrolled

Put a point on a circle of radius 1 (the unit circle), at angle θ from 5.B.1. As θ grows, the point travels around. Its height above the center goes up to 1, back down through 0, down to −1, and back. That height is called the sine of θ, written sin θ. Plot it against θ, and the circle unrolls into a wave.

The point’s across position, left or right of center, is the cosine, cos θ. It makes the very same wave shape, just starting at its top instead of its middle. (5.B.2 explores sine and cosine on the circle in depth.)

3The parts of a wave

Real waves aren’t always exactly 1 tall, centered on 0, and 2π long. You can reshape the basic sine wave in four ways, and each has a name:

  1. Amplitude A: how far the wave swings above and below its middle. A wave with amplitude 2 is twice as tall. (Stretching up and down, like 1.B.2.)
  2. Midline D: the level the wave swings around. Raising D lifts the whole wave (a slide, like 1.B.1).
  3. Period P: the length of one full cycle, from one peak to the next. Its flip side is frequency: how many cycles fit in one unit, f = 1 ÷ P.
  4. Phase shift C: how far the wave is slid left or right. A sine wave normally starts at its midline going up at x = 0; with a shift of C, it starts there at x = C.

4Cosine is sine, slid over

In the unrolling explorer, trace both waves. Cosine reaches its peak at θ = 0, while sine gets there a quarter turn later, at θ = π/2. They’re the same wave, a quarter period apart: cos θ = sin(θ + π/2). In the builder, the “Cosine” preset makes this exact wave with a phase shift of C = −π/2.

A common mistake is to think the minus sign means “slide right.” Inside the parentheses it’s the other way around: sin(x − C) slides the wave right by C, because the wave needs x to be C bigger to reach the same point. So sin(x + π/2) = sin(x − (−π/2)) slides left.

5Using the repeat

Here is the superpower of periodic functions: if you know one full cycle, you know everything. Suppose a pattern has period 12. Then its value at 30 is the same as at 30 − 12 = 18, and the same as at 18 − 12 = 6. Subtract whole periods until you land inside the first cycle. That’s how sailors predict high tide days ahead, and how astronomers know exactly when the sun will rise next year. Periodic doesn’t have to mean wavy, either: a blinking light, a traffic signal, or the days of the week (period 7) all repeat.

6Now, in symbols

The builder used four letters: amplitude A, period P, phase shift C and midline D. Mathematicians usually write the wave with a fifth letter, B, that packs the period inside the parentheses:

\[ y = A \sin\!\big(B\,(x - C)\big) + D, \qquad B = \frac{2\pi}{P} \]
The basic wave \(y = \sin x\) has \(A = 1,\ B = 1\ (P = 2\pi),\ C = 0,\ D = 0\).

Frequency is the flip side of period:

\[ f = \frac{1}{P} = \frac{B}{2\pi} \]
A note at 440 cycles per second has period \(1/440\) of a second.

And “periodic with period \(P\)” means:

\[ f(x + P) = f(x) \quad \text{for every } x \]
Add one period and you’re back to the same value. Cosine and sine are related by \(\cos\theta = \sin(\theta + \tfrac{\pi}{2})\).

  • Later in this book: in Calculus, the slope of the sine wave turns out to be the cosine wave. In Fourier analysis, every repeating signal, from a violin note to a heartbeat, can be built by adding up sine waves of different frequencies. That idea runs music apps, phone calls and medical imaging.
  • In the world: musical pitch (frequency), loudness (amplitude), tides, seasons and daylight, the electricity in your walls (60 cycles per second in the US, 50 in much of the world), and the bounce of a spring.

8Words to know

Periodic functionA pattern that repeats exactly: f(x + P) = f(x).
CycleOne complete repeat of the pattern.
PeriodThe length of one cycle, from a peak to the next peak.
FrequencyHow many cycles fit in one unit (of time, or of x): 1 ÷ period.
AmplitudeHow far the wave swings above (or below) its midline.
MidlineThe level the wave swings around, y = D.
Phase shiftHow far the wave is slid left or right.
Sine and cosineThe height and across position of a point on the unit circle at angle θ.
SinusoidAny wave with the sine shape, whatever its amplitude, period, shift or midline.
