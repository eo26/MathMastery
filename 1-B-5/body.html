The Constant of Proportionality

In every proportional relationship, one number never changes: how much of the second amount goes with exactly one of the first. Find that number, and you can answer any question about the relationship.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Find the constant of proportionality k by asking “how much for one?”
  • Use k as a machine: multiply any amount x by k to get y.
  • Write a proportional relationship as an equation: y = k · x.
  • Tell whether a table is proportional or not, and say why.

1One number that never changes

Remember the lemonade from 1.B.4: 2 scoops of mix for every 3 cups of water. Here is part of its ratio table again, with one new column. In that column, divide the cups by the scoops in each row:

Picture

Divide across every row

Every row is a different size of the same recipe, and every row gives the same answer.

Every row gives 1½. That isn’t a coincidence. It’s the recipe itself, boiled down to one number: 1½ cups of water for every 1 scoop of mix. When two amounts are in a proportional relationship, dividing the second by the first always gives the same number. That number is called the constant of proportionality, and we write it as k.

We’ll call the first amount x (here, scoops) and the second amount y (here, cups). Then k = y ÷ x in every row.

2Find the one

Here is the most useful question you can ask about a proportional relationship: how much goes with one? The answer is k.

On a double number line you can see it happen. Start from any pair you know, say 2 scoops with 3 cups. To get down to 1 scoop, divide by 2. Whatever you do to one line, you do to the other, so divide the cups by 2 as well: 3 ÷ 2 = 1½. The cups that sit over “1 scoop” are k.

The same idea works for speed. A car drives 180 miles in 3 hours. How far in one hour? 180 ÷ 3 = 60. So k = 60 miles per hour. That’s what the speedometer shows: the constant of proportionality between time and distance.

3The × k machine

Once you know k, you don’t need the table anymore. Every x turns into its y the same way: multiply by k. Think of k as a machine. Feed in 5 scoops and out come 5 × 1½ = 7½ cups. Feed in 4 hours of driving at 60 miles per hour, and out come 240 miles.

4Now, in symbols

The explorer labeled the first amount x, the second amount y, and the number over “1” on the bottom line k. The machine turned every x into its y by multiplying by k. As an equation:

\[ y = k \cdot x \]
For the lemonade, \(y = 1\tfrac{1}{2}\,x\). For the car, \(y = 60\,x\). The equation is the machine.

And to find k from any pair you know, divide, exactly like the new column in the ratio table:

\[ k = \frac{y}{x} \]
“How much \(y\) for one \(x\).” This is why \(k\) has units like cups per scoop or miles per hour: the word “per” means “for each one.”

A proportion (from 1.B.4) says two ratios are equal, like \(\tfrac{3}{2} = \tfrac{9}{6}\). Every row of a proportional table gives you one, because every row’s \(y \div x\) is the same \(k\).

5Proportional or not?

Not every pair of amounts that grow together is proportional. Take a taxi that charges $3 just to get in, plus $2 per mile. One mile costs $5, two miles cost $7. Divide: 5 ÷ 1 = 5, but 7 ÷ 2 = 3½. The answer changes, so there is no single k. The taxi fare is not proportional to the miles.

Two quick tests for a table:

  1. Divide across every row. If y ÷ x gives the same number every time, the relationship is proportional, and that number is k.
  2. Check zero. In a proportional relationship, 0 of the first amount always goes with 0 of the second: no scoops, no cups; no driving, no miles. The taxi fails this test: at 0 miles you still pay $3.

Notice the adding trap from 1.B.4 is hiding here too. A relationship like “my age and my cousin’s age” (always 3 years apart) grows together, but by adding, not multiplying, so it isn’t proportional either.

6Where this goes

  • Next (1.B.6): plot the rows of a proportional table as points and they fall on a straight line through 0. The constant of proportionality k turns out to be how steep that line is.
  • Later in this book: k grows up into the slope of a line in Algebra and the rate of change in Calculus. The taxi, a proportional part plus a starting amount, is your first linear function that isn’t proportional. And the ×k machine is the simplest example of a function: a rule that turns every input into exactly one output.
  • In the world: speed (miles per hour), unit prices at the store (dollars per pound), recipes (cups per scoop), and fuel use (miles per gallon) are all constants of proportionality. Any time you hear the word per, look for a k.

7Words to know

Constant of proportionalityThe number k you multiply x by to get y, the same for every pair. It’s how much y goes with one x: k = y ÷ x.
Proportional relationshipTwo amounts where y ÷ x is always the same number, so y = k · x. Zero goes with zero, and doubling one doubles the other.
ProportionA statement that two ratios are equal, like 3/2 = 9/6. Any two rows of a proportional table make one.
Unit rateHow much of one amount goes with exactly 1 of the other: 1½ cups per scoop, 60 miles per hour. In a proportional relationship, the unit rate is k.
Per“For each one.” Miles per hour means miles for each one hour.
Not proportionalTwo amounts where y ÷ x changes, or 0 doesn’t go with 0, like a taxi fare with a starting fee.
