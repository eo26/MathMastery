Chapter 8 · Calculus

Integrals

An integral adds up infinitely many thin slices: the area under a curve, the distance from a speedometer, the energy from a power meter. And it turns out to be the derivative run backwards.

Basics you are here Undergraduate Masters

On this page

  • Approximate area with Riemann sums (left, right, midpoint) and define the definite integral as their limit.
  • Read area below the axis as negative (signed area), and find the average value of a function.
  • Find antiderivatives (with the constant +C) using the basic rules.
  • Use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: integrals are evaluated with antiderivatives, and the rate of change of accumulated area is the original function.

1Area as a limit of sums

A car’s speedometer reads a steady 60 km/h for 2 hours: distance = 60 × 2 = 120 km, the area of a rectangle under the flat speed graph. When the speed changes, chop the time into short pieces, pretend the speed is steady on each piece, and add up the rectangles. That is a Riemann sum. Each rectangle’s width is Δx = (b − a)/n; its height is f at the left end, the right end, or the midpoint of its piece.

More, thinner rectangles hug the curve better. The limit as n → ∞ is the definite integral, written ∫ab f(x) dx. The ∫ is a stretched S for “sum”, and dx stands for the vanishing width Δx. Where f dips below the axis, the rectangles have negative height, so that area counts as negative: the integral is signed area.

2Antiderivatives

An antiderivative of f is a function F whose derivative is f: the derivative run backwards. Since x² has derivative 2x, an antiderivative of 2x is x². But so is x² + 5, or x² − 1: shifting a graph up or down doesn’t change any slopes. So antiderivatives come as a family, F(x) + C, with an arbitrary constant of integration C. The family is written ∫ f(x) dx, an indefinite integral (no limits on the ∫).

3The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Look again at the running total A(x) in the explorer. When you move x a tiny step dx to the right, the area grows by a thin strip of height f(x) and width dx. So the area grows at the rate f(x): A′(x) = f(x). The accumulated area is an antiderivative of the function. That is the first half of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and it ties the two halves of calculus together: integrating and differentiating undo each other.

The second half is the practical one. If F is any antiderivative of f, then

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a). \]

No rectangles needed: ∫02 x² dx = 2³/3 − 0³/3 = 8/3. The constant C doesn’t matter, because it cancels in the subtraction. In motion terms: the area under a velocity graph is the change in position.

    4Signed area and average value

    Area above the axis counts positive, below counts negative, and the integral is the net result. ∫02π sin x dx = 0: the hump and the dip cancel. If you want the total area regardless of sign, integrate |f| instead.

    The average value of f on [a, b] is the height of the rectangle with the same width and the same (signed) area: (1/(b − a))∫ab f(x) dx. Picture the region as sand and level it flat: the flat height is the average. A day’s average temperature or a trip’s average speed are exactly this.

    5Rate in, total out

    Whenever you know a rate and want a total, you integrate. Speed (km per hour) over time gives distance. Power (kilowatts) over time gives energy (kilowatt-hours, what the electricity bill counts). Flow (litres per minute) over time gives volume, with outflow counting negative. The units tell you: the area of a rectangle with height in km/h and width in hours is in km.

    6Traps and non-examples

    Compare

    Easy to get wrong

    ✕ Integral ≠ total area

    ∫02π sin x dx = 0, but the shaded area is 4. The integral nets the parts below the axis against those above.

    ✕ Forgetting to divide by the new power

    ∫ x² dx is x³/3, not x³: the derivative of x³ is 3x², three times too big. Always check by differentiating your answer.

    ✕ Dropping the +C

    x², x² + 2 and x² − 1 all have derivative 2x. An indefinite integral is the whole family; only a definite integral (where C cancels) gives one number.

    7Now, in symbols

    \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n} \]
    A Riemann sum and its limit; xi* is a point in the i-th piece (left end, right end or midpoint).
    \[ \int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C\ (n \ne -1), \qquad \int \cos x\,dx = \sin x + C, \qquad \int e^x\,dx = e^x + C \]
    \[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x), \qquad \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \text{ where } F' = f \]
    The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, both halves.
    \[ \text{average of } f \text{ on } [a,b] = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_a^b f + \int_b^c f = \int_a^c f \]

    Riemann sums are what a computer actually does when there’s no formula, for example with measured data:

    import numpy as np                              # numerical arrays
import sympy as sp                              # exact symbolic answers

n = 1000                                        # number of rectangles
dx = 2 / n                                      # their width on [0, 2]
mids = np.arange(n) * dx + dx / 2               # the midpoint of each piece
print(np.sum(mids**2 * dx))                     # 2.666666...: the midpoint sum for x² on [0, 2]

x = sp.symbols('x')                             # exact version
print(sp.integrate(x**2, (x, 0, 2)))            # 8/3, from the antiderivative x³/3
print(sp.integrate(sp.sin(x), x))               # -cos(x): sympy leaves out the + C

speeds = np.array([0, 30, 55, 60, 60, 40, 0])   # km/h, read every 10 minutes
print(np.trapezoid(speeds, dx=10/60))           # ≈ 40.8 km: distance from a speed log (trapezoid rule)
