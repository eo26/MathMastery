Equivalent Ratios

A ratio is a recipe, not a pair of amounts. Make more of it by multiplying both parts by the same number, and the result tastes exactly the same.

On this page

  • Read a ratio three ways: 2 : 3, “2 to 3”, and 2/3.
  • Make equivalent ratios by multiplying (or dividing) both parts by the same number.
  • Use a ratio table and a double number line to find missing amounts.
  • Spot the adding trap: why adding the same amount to both parts changes the ratio.

1What a ratio says

Here is a lemonade recipe: 2 scoops of lemon mix for every 3 cups of water. That “for every” is the heart of a ratio. It compares two amounts by saying how much of one goes with how much of the other.

There are three common ways to write it, and they all mean the same thing:

2 : 3   ·   2 to 3   ·   2/3
Read all three as “2 scoops for every 3 cups.” The order matters: scoops first, cups second.

We’ll call the two parts of the recipe a (scoops) and b (cups). One batch of the recipe looks like this:

2More lemonade, same taste

One batch is not enough for a party. So you make the recipe again, and again. Two batches use 4 scoops and 6 cups. Three batches use 6 scoops and 9 cups. The amounts change, but every sip tastes exactly the same, because every cup of water still has the same amount of mix in it.

Ratios that describe the same mix are called equivalent ratios. 2 : 3, 4 : 6 and 6 : 9 are all equivalent. Each one is the same recipe, made a different number of times. The number of batches is n, and making n batches multiplies both parts by n.

3Reading a ratio table

A ratio table lists equivalent ratios in rows. The rule for moving between rows is always the same: whatever you multiply one column by, multiply the other column by the same number. Going from row 1 to row 3 multiplies both columns by 3. Going from row 4 back up to row 2 divides both by 2.

Notice the half batch at the top of the table. Dividing both parts by 2 gives 1 scoop for 1½ cups. Fractions are perfectly good amounts in a ratio, and that row tastes exactly like all the others.

When two amounts always come in equivalent ratios like this, every row a stretched or shrunk copy of the recipe, we say they are in a proportional relationship. Scoops and cups in this recipe are proportional: double the scoops and the cups double too.

4Reading a double number line

A double number line puts two number lines on top of each other: scoops above, cups below. They’re spaced so that amounts that belong together sit directly on top of each other. 2 scoops sits over 3 cups, 4 over 6, 6 over 9.

It’s the number line from 1.B.1, doubled. And look at the spacing: every step along the top line is 2 scoops, while the same step along the bottom line is 3 cups. The bottom line is the top line, stretched. You’ll make that stretch precise on the next page.

5The adding trap

Here is the most common mistake people make with ratios. To make more lemonade, someone adds 1 more scoop and 1 more cup, so 2 : 3 becomes 3 : 4. It feels fair, because they added the same amount to both. But it is not the same recipe.

Why? In the original, there are 2 scoops for 3 cups, so the water outnumbers the mix by a lot, relatively speaking. Adding 1 to each makes the two amounts more alike, so the lemonade gets stronger. Keep adding and it gets stronger still. Try it:

7Now, in symbols

In the batch builder, the recipe was labeled a : b (scoops : cups) and the number of batches was n. Making n batches multiplies both parts:

\[ a : b \;=\; (n \times a) : (n \times b) \]
With the lemonade and \(n = 3\): \(2 : 3 = 6 : 9\). This works for any \(n\) bigger than 0, including fractions like \(n = \tfrac{1}{2}\).

A statement that two ratios are equal has its own name: a proportion. Written with fractions, the lemonade proportion is

\[ \frac{2}{3} = \frac{6}{9} \]
“2 is to 3 as 6 is to 9.” Every row of the ratio table gives you another true proportion.

A quick way to check a proportion: multiply across the diagonals. If the ratios are equivalent, the two products match.

\[ \frac{2}{3} \;\;\text{and}\;\; \frac{6}{9}: \qquad 2 \times 9 = 18 \qquad 3 \times 6 = 18 \;\;✓ \]
They match because the same multiplier (\(n = 3\)) hides in both products: \(2 \times (3 \times 3)\) and \(3 \times (3 \times 2)\). With the adding trap, \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) and \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) give \(2 \times 4 = 8\) but \(3 \times 3 = 9\), so they are not equivalent.

8Where this goes

  • Next (1.B.5): divide the cups by the scoops in any row of the ratio table and you get the same answer every time: 1½ cups per scoop. That never-changing number is called the constant of proportionality.
  • Later in this book: plot the rows of a ratio table as points and they line up in a straight line through 0 (1.B.6). In Geometry, shapes whose sides are in equivalent ratios are called similar: same shape, different size. And multiplying every part of something by the same number, called scaling, is one of the basic moves of Linear Algebra.
  • In the world: recipes, mixing paint colors, the scale on a map or a model car, and how fast something moves (miles for every hour) are all ratios. Whenever you make more of something and want it to stay “the same,” you’re using equivalent ratios.

9Words to know

RatioA comparison of two amounts that says how much of one goes with how much of the other: 2 : 3.
Terms of a ratioThe two numbers in it. In 2 : 3, the terms are 2 and 3.
Equivalent ratiosRatios that describe the same mix: 2 : 3, 4 : 6, 6 : 9.
Ratio tableA table of equivalent ratios, where every row is the same recipe at a different size.
Double number lineTwo number lines lined up so that amounts that go together sit on top of each other.
ProportionA statement that two ratios are equal: 2/3 = 6/9.
Proportional relationshipTwo amounts that always come in equivalent ratios, like scoops and cups in every row of the ratio table. Double one and the other doubles too.
Constant of proportionalityThe one number you multiply the first amount by to get the second, the same in every row. For the lemonade: cups = 1½ × scoops. (Explored fully in 1.B.5.)
