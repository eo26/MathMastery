Primes, Factors and Multiples

Some numbers of tiles make lots of rectangles; some make only a single row. The ones that can’t be broken up, the primes, are the atoms every whole number is built from.

1Factors are the sides of rectangles

Set out 12 chairs in equal rows with none left over. You can make 1 row of 12, 2 rows of 6, 3 rows of 4, or turn any of those sideways: 4 rows of 3, 6 of 2, 12 of 1. Try 5 rows and you get 5 rows of 2 with 2 left over, a remainder. The numbers that work, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12, are the factors of 12: the whole numbers that go into 12 exactly. We also say 12 is divisible by 3, or that 3 divides 12.

Factors come in factor pairs, the two sides of one rectangle: 1 × 12, 2 × 6, 3 × 4. Flip it around and you get the multiples: 12 is a multiple of 3 because 12 = 3 × 4. The multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, … and they go on forever, while the factors of 12 stop at 12. Factors divide into a number; multiples are what you get by multiplying it up.

2Prime or composite

Try 13 chairs. One row of 13 works, and so does 13 rows of 1, but nothing else does. A whole number bigger than 1 whose only factors are 1 and itself is prime: it makes exactly one rectangle, a single line. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 are primes. A number with more factors, like 12, is composite: it can be built from smaller numbers multiplied together.

The number 1 is neither. It has only one factor, itself, and a prime needs exactly two. Section 4 shows why leaving 1 out matters. 2 is the only even prime, since every other even number has 2 as a factor. But “odd” doesn’t mean “prime”: 9, 15, 21 and 91 are all odd and all composite.

You don’t have to try every row count to test a number. Factors pair up, a small one with a big one, and the pairs meet in the middle at √n. 36 has the pair 6 × 6, and the pairs before it (1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9) are the same rectangles as the ones after, turned sideways. So if nothing up to √n divides n, nothing bigger will either. To show 97 is prime, try 2 through 9 and stop. That middle pair also explains a pattern: a square number like 36 has an odd number of factors, because 6 pairs with itself.

3The sieve, and quick divisibility checks

Over 2,000 years ago, Eratosthenes found every prime up to a limit without testing each number. Circle 2, then cross out every multiple of 2 after it. The next number not crossed out, 3, must be prime; cross out its multiples. Then 5, then 7. To reach 100 you can stop after 7, because the next prime, 11, has 11 × 11 = 121 > 100: any composite number up to 100 has a factor of 7 or less and is already crossed out. Everything left is prime. This is the sieve of Eratosthenes; press “Play: sieve to 100” above.

For one big number, these divisibility rules save time. They work because we write numbers in tens:

  • 2, 5, 10: look at the last digit (even; 0 or 5; 0). Every ten is already divisible by them, so only the ones digit matters.
  • 4: look at the last two digits. Every hundred is divisible by 4.
  • 3 and 9: add the digits. 10 is one more than 9, 100 is one more than 99, so a number and its digit sum leave the same remainder when divided by 3 or 9.
  • 6: passes the tests for both 2 and 3.

4Every number has one prime recipe

A composite number splits into two smaller factors. If either of those is composite, split it again. Keep going and you end at primes, which can’t split. Drawn out, this is a factor tree:

\[ 72 = 8 \times 9 = (2 \times 4) \times (3 \times 3) = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 2^3 \times 3^2. \]

Writing a number as a product of primes is its prime factorization. Here’s the surprising part: start with 72 = 6 × 12 or 72 = 2 × 36 and the tree looks different, but the primes at the bottom are always three 2s and two 3s. That fact is the fundamental theorem of arithmetic: every whole number bigger than 1 is a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from order. Primes are the atoms; every whole number is a molecule with one formula.

This is why 1 isn’t called prime. If it were, 6 = 2 × 3 = 1 × 2 × 3 = 1 × 1 × 2 × 3 would have endless recipes, and the theorem would need an exception.

5Greatest common factor and least common multiple

Two numbers can share factors. The factors of 18 are 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18; the factors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24. Their common factors are 1, 2, 3, 6, and the greatest common factor (GCF, also called the greatest common divisor, gcd) is 6. Their common multiples are 72, 144, 216, …, and the least common multiple (LCM) is 72.

Listing works for small numbers. Prime recipes work for any. Put the primes of 18 = 2 × 3 × 3 and 24 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 into two overlapping circles, with the primes they share (one 2 and one 3) in the middle:

  • The GCF is the product of the middle: 2 × 3 = 6. It’s the biggest number made only from primes both numbers have.
  • The LCM is the product of everything, counting the middle once: 3 × (2 × 3) × 2 × 2 = 72. It’s the smallest number containing both recipes.

Multiply them and every prime gets counted exactly as often as in 18 × 24, so GCF × LCM = 18 × 24 always holds. When two numbers share no prime at all, like 8 and 15, the GCF is 1 and they are relatively prime (or coprime); then the LCM is simply their product.

6Which one does the story need?

Both show up all the time in everyday problems, and the trick is telling them apart.

  • GCF: splitting into equal groups or the biggest equal pieces. 18 pencils and 24 stickers into identical party bags with nothing left: at most GCF = 6 bags. Cutting two ribbons into the longest equal pieces. Covering a floor with the biggest square tiles. The answer is never bigger than the smaller number.
  • LCM: waiting for repeating things to line up. Lights blinking every 4 and every 6 seconds blink together every 12 seconds. Buses, laps, gear teeth, rhythms, hot dogs in packs of 10 and buns in packs of 8. The answer is never smaller than the bigger number.

You’ve met both already with fractions (1.B.3). Simplify 18/24 by dividing top and bottom by the GCF: 18/24 = 3/4. Add 1/4 + 1/6 by rewriting both in twelfths, because 12 is the LCM of the denominators: 3/12 + 2/12 = 5/12.

7The primes never run out

Primes get rarer as numbers grow: 25 of the numbers up to 100 are prime, but only 168 up to 1,000. Do they ever stop? Around 300 BC, Euclid showed they don’t, with an argument short enough to fit on a napkin.

Suppose someone hands you a list of primes and claims it’s all of them. Multiply them all together and add 1. Call the result N. Dividing N by any prime on the list leaves remainder 1, so no prime on the list divides N. But N is bigger than 1, so it has at least one prime factor, and that prime isn’t on the list. Every list is missing a prime, so there are infinitely many primes.

Careful: N itself needn’t be prime. With the list 2, 3, 5, 7, N = 211, which is prime. Add 11 and 13 and N = 30,031, which isn’t. A few lines of Python find its prime factor:

from math import prod                       # prod() multiplies a list of numbers together
def smallest_factor(n):                     # the smallest prime that divides n
    d = 2                                   # start testing at 2
    while d * d <= n:                       # only test up to √n: factors pair up around it
        if n % d == 0:                      # d goes into n with no remainder...
            return d                        # ...so it's the smallest prime factor
        d += 1                              # otherwise try the next number
    return n                                # nothing up to √n divides n, so n is prime
primes = [2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13]               # a list someone claims is "all the primes"
N = prod(primes) + 1                        # multiply them all and add 1: 30031
p = smallest_factor(N)                      # N must have some prime factor
print(N, p, N // p)                         # 30031 59 509: 30031 = 59 × 509
print([N % q for q in primes])              # [1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]: no listed prime divides N

59 and 509 are both primes missing from the list, exactly as Euclid’s argument promised.

8Traps

Compare

Easy to get wrong

✕ 1 is prime

A prime has exactly two factors, 1 and itself. 1 has only one. If 1 counted, no number would have a single prime recipe: 6 = 2 × 3 = 1 × 2 × 3 = …

✕ LCM = A × B

Lights every 4 s and every 6 s do blink together at 4 × 6 = 24 s, but they already met at 12 s. A × B is a common multiple; it’s the least one only when A and B share no prime.

✕ GCF and LCM swapped

The GCF goes into both numbers, so it’s at most the smaller one. The LCM is a multiple of both, so it’s at least the larger one. If your “GCF” is bigger than either number, you found a multiple.

9Now, in symbols

For whole numbers, a divides b, written a | b, when b = a × k for some whole number k. Then a is a factor of b and b is a multiple of a. Otherwise division leaves a remainder: b = a × q + r with 0 < r < a.

Every whole number n > 1 has exactly one prime factorization (the fundamental theorem of arithmetic):

\[ n = p_1^{e_1}\, p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}, \qquad p_1 < p_2 < \dots < p_k \text{ prime}, \quad e_i \ge 1. \]

Write both numbers with the same primes, using exponent 0 for a missing prime, say A = ∏ pai and B = ∏ pbi. Then the shared part takes the smaller exponent and the LCM the larger:

\[ \gcd(A,B) = \prod p_i^{\min(a_i,\,b_i)}, \qquad \operatorname{lcm}(A,B) = \prod p_i^{\max(a_i,\,b_i)}, \qquad \gcd(A,B)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(A,B) = A\,B. \]

Since min + max of two exponents is their sum, the last identity follows. A number n is prime exactly when no prime p ≤ √n divides it. And Euclid’s argument: for any primes p₁, …, pk, the number N = p₁p₂⋯pk + 1 leaves remainder 1 on division by each, so its prime factors are new.
